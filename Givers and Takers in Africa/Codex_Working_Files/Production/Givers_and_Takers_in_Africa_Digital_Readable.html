CHAPTER ONE

UNDERSTANDING GIVERS AND TAKERS: THE FOUNDATIONS OF HUMAN BEHAVIOUR

1.1 Introduction

Human societies are characterised by different patterns of behaviour, motivations and relationships. While some individuals devote considerable portions of their lives to improving the welfare of others, others concentrate primarily on advancing their personal interests, sometimes without regard for the consequences of their actions on society.

These contrasting orientations provide the foundation for understanding the concepts of givers and takers.

The distinction is illustrated by a popular reflection:

"My father said there were two kinds of people in the world: givers and takers. The takers may eat better, but the givers sleep better."

Although the statement is expressed in simple language, it raises important philosophical, psychological, sociological, economic and political questions.

What does it mean to be a giver? Why do some individuals sacrifice personal comfort to improve the welfare of others? Why do some people accumulate wealth and power without considering their responsibilities to society? Why do individuals entrusted with public resources sometimes regard public office as an opportunity for personal enrichment?

These questions are particularly relevant to Africa, where the challenges of poverty, inequality, unemployment, corruption and institutional weaknesses make the relationship between individual behaviour and collective development especially important.

1.2 Who Is a Giver?

A giver is an individual or institution that willingly contributes resources, knowledge, time, skills, opportunities or other forms of assistance to improve the welfare of others.

Giving is not restricted to financial donations. It includes mentoring, teaching, volunteering, providing employment, sharing knowledge, protecting vulnerable people, promoting justice and creating opportunities for others.

A teacher who devotes time to helping disadvantaged children is a giver.

A public servant who ensures that government resources reach their intended beneficiaries is a giver.

An entrepreneur who creates employment opportunities and treats employees fairly is a giver.

A political leader who invests public resources in education, healthcare, infrastructure and economic opportunities is a giver.

A parent who sacrifices personal comfort to secure the future of children is equally a giver.

Giving, therefore, is a broad expression of social responsibility.

It reflects the understanding that human existence is interconnected and that individual success becomes more meaningful when it contributes to the advancement of others.

1.3 Who Is a Taker?

A taker is an individual whose principal concern is personal advantage, accumulation or satisfaction, frequently without sufficient consideration for the interests and welfare of others.

Taking is not necessarily wrong. Individuals have legitimate needs and are entitled to receive compensation for their labour, enjoy the fruits of their investments and pursue personal ambitions.

The problem arises when taking becomes exploitative, excessive, dishonest or destructive of the common good.

Examples include:

A public official who diverts public funds into private accounts.

A politician who distributes public resources primarily to secure personal political loyalty.

An employer who exploits workers while accumulating excessive profits.

A businessperson who manipulates consumers through dishonest practices.

A community leader who monopolises opportunities meant for collective benefit.

An individual who continually receives assistance but refuses to contribute despite having the capacity to do so.

The taker is not necessarily someone who possesses material wealth. A person may be poor and still display an exploitative attitude.

Similarly, a wealthy person may be a generous giver who contributes substantially to society.

Consequently, the distinction is fundamentally about character, motivation, conduct and social responsibility rather than economic status.

1.4 The Economics of Giving and Taking

From an economic perspective, giving and taking influence the distribution and utilisation of resources.

A productive society requires individuals and institutions to contribute to the creation of wealth while ensuring that the benefits of economic activity are distributed fairly.

Giving can produce positive economic externalities. When individuals invest in education, healthcare, research, infrastructure, entrepreneurship and community development, the benefits frequently extend beyond the immediate beneficiaries.

For example, educating a young person does not benefit that individual alone. It can improve household income, increase productivity, strengthen institutions and contribute to national economic growth.

Conversely, excessive taking can undermine economic development.

When public resources are diverted, funds intended for schools, hospitals, roads, electricity and water supply become unavailable for their intended purposes.

When businesses exploit consumers or workers, trust declines and economic relationships become less productive.

When political leaders distribute opportunities according to personal loyalty rather than competence, national productivity suffers.

The economic distinction is therefore between productive contribution and extractive behaviour.

1.5 The Moral and Psychological Dimensions

Giving is associated with empathy, compassion, generosity, responsibility and concern for the welfare of others.

It may also provide psychological satisfaction because individuals recognise that their actions have contributed positively to society.

The satisfaction of helping another person cannot always be measured in financial terms.

A retired public administrator who mentors younger officers, a successful businessperson who supports vocational education and a community leader who mediates disputes may receive no immediate financial reward. Nevertheless, their contributions can produce lasting social benefits.

Taking, when driven by greed and excessive self-interest, may produce temporary material satisfaction but can undermine personal relationships and social trust.

This helps explain the moral significance of the statement that takers may eat better while givers sleep better.

The expression is not a universal economic law. Rather, it highlights the difference between material gratification and peace of conscience.

1.6 Givers and Takers in Nigeria

Nigeria provides a particularly important environment for examining the relationship between giving and taking.

The country possesses enormous human and natural resources, entrepreneurial energy, cultural diversity and traditions of communal assistance.

Nigerians frequently demonstrate generosity through family support, community associations, religious organisations, philanthropy, cooperative societies and assistance to disadvantaged persons.

However, the country also faces serious governance challenges involving corruption, patronage, resource diversion and the misuse of public authority.

Afrobarometer reported in May 2025 that 80% of surveyed Nigerians believed corruption had increased somewhat or a lot during the preceding year. The report also highlighted widespread concerns about bribery and the limited confidence citizens had in reporting corruption without retaliation. (Afrobarometer, 6 May 2025).

These findings illustrate the importance of ethical leadership and institutional accountability.

They should not obscure the contributions of honest public servants, responsible political leaders, hardworking entrepreneurs, teachers, healthcare professionals and ordinary citizens who continue to contribute to national development.

The central challenge is to ensure that the culture of contribution becomes stronger than the culture of extraction.

1.7 The Implications for African Development

Africa's development challenges cannot be addressed through financial resources and technical expertise alone.

They also require appropriate values, responsible leadership and institutions that encourage individuals to contribute to the common good.

A society in which people are primarily interested in what they can obtain from public office, government programmes or community resources will struggle to achieve sustainable development.

Conversely, a society that rewards integrity, productive contribution, innovation and social responsibility creates better conditions for inclusive prosperity.

The transformation of African societies therefore requires a deliberate movement from excessive self-interest towards responsible individual and collective action.

1.8 Conclusion

Givers and takers represent two contrasting orientations to human existence.

The giver recognises that personal advancement and collective welfare can reinforce one another.

The destructive taker, on the other hand, seeks personal advantage without adequate regard for the consequences of such behaviour on others.

For Nigeria and other African countries, the challenge is not to eliminate personal ambition or legitimate self-interest. Rather, it is to reconcile individual aspirations with social responsibility.

The ultimate objective should be to create societies in which wealth creation, leadership, public administration and individual success contribute meaningfully to the common good.

Central Lesson: A person's worth should not be measured exclusively by what he or she possesses, but also by what that person contributes to the lives of others.

CHAPTER TWO

THE AFRICAN PHILOSOPHY OF COMMUNAL LIVING, SOLIDARITY AND RECIPROCITY

2.1 Introduction

African societies possess longstanding traditions of communal living, mutual assistance, cooperation and collective responsibility.

Before the emergence of modern bureaucratic institutions and contemporary political systems, many African communities depended substantially on informal arrangements through which individuals and families supported one another.

These arrangements included communal farming, collective construction of houses, assistance during childbirth, support for widows and orphans, contributions towards marriages and funerals, and the settlement of disputes through traditional institutions.

Such practices demonstrate that giving is not foreign to African societies. On the contrary, it is deeply embedded in many African social traditions.

The challenge is to understand how these values can be preserved, strengthened and adapted to contemporary political and economic circumstances.

2.2 The Philosophy of Ubuntu

One of the most widely discussed African philosophical traditions is Ubuntu, associated particularly with Southern African societies.

Ubuntu is commonly expressed through the idea that a person becomes a person through other people.

It emphasises humanity, compassion, mutual respect, solidarity and interconnectedness.

Although Ubuntu is particularly associated with Southern Africa, its central principles resonate with communal traditions across other parts of the continent.

In Nigeria, similar ideas can be identified in the expressions of communal responsibility and mutual assistance found among different ethnic groups.

Among the Igbo, the concept of Igwebuike emphasises the strength associated with collective unity.

Among the Yoruba, the principle of Omoluabi emphasises good character, integrity, responsibility and respect for others.

Among the Edo people, communal relationships and mutual obligations have traditionally played important roles in family and community organisation.

Across different African societies, the underlying message is that individuals do not exist independently of their social environments.

Their welfare is connected to the welfare of families, communities and society.

2.3 The Extended Family as an Institution of Giving

The African extended family has historically functioned as an important institution of social protection.

Unlike the nuclear family, which generally concentrates on parents and their children, the extended family may include grandparents, uncles, aunts, cousins, nephews, nieces and other relatives.

This structure provides opportunities for sharing responsibilities.

A financially successful family member may assist younger relatives with education, employment, healthcare and housing.

An employed person may support elderly parents or relatives who are unable to work.

A family may collectively contribute towards the education of an exceptionally talented but financially disadvantaged child.

These practices represent forms of informal social investment.

However, the extended family system can also create excessive financial obligations.

Successful individuals may be subjected to demands that exceed their capacity. In some circumstances, assistance may encourage dependency rather than productive self-reliance.

The objective should therefore be to preserve solidarity while promoting responsibility, empowerment and accountability.

2.4 Communal Labour and Collective Responsibility

Traditional African communities developed different arrangements for collective labour.

Community members worked together to clear farmland, construct houses, repair roads, maintain public spaces and organise communal activities.

Such cooperation reduced the burden on individuals and enabled communities to undertake projects that would have been difficult to accomplish independently.

These practices demonstrate the developmental significance of giving.

When people contribute labour, knowledge, materials and organisational skills towards common projects, the entire community benefits.

Modern local governments and development agencies can adapt these principles through community participation, cooperative enterprises and participatory budgeting.

The important lesson is that development becomes more sustainable when beneficiaries participate in its planning, implementation and maintenance.

2.5 Religious Organisations and Philanthropy

Religious organisations have historically played important roles in African charitable activities.

Christian churches, Islamic organisations and other religious communities provide assistance to disadvantaged households, support educational institutions, organise medical outreach programmes and offer relief during emergencies.

Religious teachings across different traditions emphasise compassion, charity, generosity and concern for vulnerable persons.

In Christianity, giving is associated with love of one's neighbour and responsibility towards the poor.

In Islam, Zakat and Sadaqah provide important frameworks for charitable giving and social support.

These traditions demonstrate the capacity of religious institutions to mobilise resources for social development.

However, religious giving should be accompanied by transparency, accountability and responsible stewardship.

The collection of charitable contributions should not become an opportunity for exploitation by religious leaders or institutions.

2.6 Reciprocity: Giving Without Exploitation

An important feature of communal relationships is reciprocity.

Reciprocity means that individuals recognise mutual obligations and responsibilities within their social relationships.

It does not necessarily mean that every act of generosity must be repaid immediately or in exactly the same form.

A person may assist a neighbour today, knowing that another person may provide assistance to someone else tomorrow.

The arrangement creates a network of mutual support.

However, reciprocity must not become an excuse for exploitative behaviour.

A person who receives assistance should not automatically regard the giver as permanently responsible for meeting all personal needs.

Similarly, a giver should not use generosity to control, intimidate or manipulate beneficiaries.

Healthy reciprocity is based on mutual respect and dignity.

2.7 The Challenges of Modernisation

Urbanisation, migration, unemployment, economic pressures and changing family structures have transformed communal relationships in many African countries.

People increasingly live away from their extended families and traditional communities.

The demands of modern employment and urban living may reduce the time and resources available for communal activities.

Nevertheless, the spirit of giving has not disappeared.

It has adapted through professional associations, alumni organisations, community development associations, hometown unions, cooperatives, charitable foundations and digital fundraising networks.

These organisations provide opportunities to extend traditional solidarity into modern institutional arrangements.

2.8 Conclusion

African communal traditions provide an important foundation for building giving societies.

The values of solidarity, reciprocity, compassion and collective responsibility can contribute significantly to poverty reduction and sustainable development.

However, traditional generosity must be strengthened by modern principles of transparency, productivity, fairness and accountability.

The objective should be to create a society in which people assist one another without encouraging dependency, exploitation or abuse of communal resources.

Central Lesson: African societies possess a rich heritage of giving. Their developmental challenge is to transform this heritage into effective institutions that promote shared prosperity.

CHAPTER THREE

THE PSYCHOLOGY AND SOCIOLOGY OF GIVING AND TAKING

3.1 Introduction

The behaviour of individuals within society is influenced by psychological, social, cultural, economic and institutional factors.

Some people derive satisfaction from helping others, while others are primarily motivated by personal advancement, material acquisition and the exercise of power.

Understanding these motivations is important for explaining why some individuals become generous contributors to society while others engage in exploitative behaviour.

The distinction between givers and takers should therefore not be examined exclusively from a moral perspective. It also requires an understanding of human psychology and social relationships.

3.2 Human Motivation and Self-Interest

Human beings have different needs and motivations.

They seek food, shelter, security, recognition, achievement, belonging and personal fulfilment.

These needs influence how individuals behave towards others.

A person who is struggling to meet basic needs may naturally prioritise personal survival.

Another individual who has achieved economic security may become more willing to support relatives, communities and charitable causes.

However, material circumstances do not automatically determine generosity.

There are wealthy individuals who are exceptionally generous and poor individuals who willingly share their limited resources.

Likewise, some wealthy individuals become increasingly acquisitive, while some people with modest incomes demonstrate extraordinary compassion.

The distinction therefore involves personal values, upbringing, experiences and social expectations.

3.3 The Psychology of Empathy

Empathy is the capacity to understand or appreciate the feelings, experiences and circumstances of another person.

It plays an important role in encouraging generosity.

An empathetic individual is more likely to recognise the suffering of disadvantaged people and become motivated to provide assistance.

For example, an employer who understands the economic difficulties facing workers may adopt fairer employment practices.

A political leader who appreciates the suffering of unemployed young people may prioritise employment creation.

A teacher who recognises the disadvantages experienced by poor students may provide additional guidance and support.

Empathy encourages people to look beyond personal circumstances and recognise their obligations towards others.

3.4 The Influence of Childhood Socialisation

Family upbringing plays a significant role in the development of attitudes towards giving and taking.

Children observe how parents treat relatives, neighbours, domestic workers, employees and strangers.

A child raised in an environment where parents regularly share resources, assist disadvantaged persons and demonstrate respect for others may gradually develop similar attitudes.

Conversely, children who consistently observe dishonesty, exploitation, selfishness and disregard for others may come to regard such conduct as normal.

This does not mean that childhood experiences permanently determine adult behaviour.

Education, personal experiences, religious instruction, mentorship and exposure to positive role models can change individual attitudes.

Nevertheless, the family remains one of the earliest and most influential institutions of moral development.

3.5 Social Recognition and the Desire for Status

Giving and taking may also be influenced by the desire for recognition.

Some individuals contribute to society because they genuinely believe in helping others.

Others may give because they desire public recognition, political support or social prestige.

Such motivations are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

A philanthropist may genuinely wish to assist disadvantaged children while also appreciating the recognition associated with charitable activities.

The important question is whether the contribution produces genuine and sustainable benefits.

Similarly, some individuals take advantage of public positions because they associate wealth accumulation with social status and prestige.

In societies where material possessions are treated as the principal indicators of success, individuals may be encouraged to pursue wealth without sufficient attention to how it is acquired.

This creates an important challenge for African societies.

They must redefine success to include integrity, contribution, productivity and social responsibility.

3.6 The Sociology of Social Exchange

Social exchange theory provides a useful framework for understanding relationships between individuals and groups.

People engage in social relationships involving different forms of exchange.

These may include money, labour, knowledge, affection, recognition, assistance and influence.

In healthy relationships, exchanges are often governed by expectations of fairness and reciprocity.

However, relationships become exploitative when one party consistently receives benefits without making reasonable contributions or when one party uses superior power to impose unfair conditions on another.

This can occur within families, workplaces, political organisations and economic institutions.

For example, a political patron may provide financial assistance to a community in exchange for unquestioning political loyalty.

An employer may demand excessive work without providing fair compensation.

A powerful individual may monopolise community resources while presenting personal enrichment as collective development.

Understanding these relationships helps distinguish genuine generosity from strategic manipulation.

3.7 The Culture of Entitlement

A culture of entitlement develops when individuals believe they are permanently entitled to benefits without corresponding responsibilities.

This attitude may manifest in different ways.

Some political officeholders believe that public resources are available for personal enjoyment.

Some citizens believe that government should provide benefits without any expectation of responsible citizenship.

Some relatives may expect successful family members to provide unlimited financial support.

Some employees may demand advancement without demonstrating competence or commitment.

The problem is not the legitimate expectation of fair treatment or social protection.

Rather, it is the rejection of reciprocal responsibility.

A sustainable society must balance rights with obligations.

Citizens have rights to public services, security and justice. They also have responsibilities to obey legitimate laws, pay appropriate taxes, protect public property and contribute to community welfare.

3.8 The Role of Institutions in Shaping Behaviour

Individual behaviour is influenced by the incentives and rules established by society.

Where corruption is rewarded, accountability is weak and public resources are poorly protected, individuals may be encouraged to engage in taking behaviour.

Where merit, transparency, integrity and productive contribution are rewarded, positive behaviour becomes more attractive.

This is particularly important in public administration.

A civil service that rewards competence and ethical conduct is more likely to attract individuals who value public service.

An institution that tolerates bribery, nepotism and abuse of authority may discourage honest employees.

Therefore, the transformation from a taking society to a giving society cannot depend exclusively on moral appeals.

It requires institutional reforms that reward responsible behaviour and penalise exploitation.

3.9 Conclusion

The psychology and sociology of giving and taking reveal that human behaviour is influenced by multiple factors.

Empathy, childhood socialisation, cultural values, social recognition, economic circumstances and institutional incentives all play important roles.

The task of national development is therefore to create environments that encourage generosity, responsible self-interest and productive contribution.

Nigeria and other African countries must invest not only in physical infrastructure and economic resources but also in character development, ethical education and institutional integrity.

Central Lesson: A giving society cannot be built through sermons alone. It requires families, schools, workplaces, religious organisations and government institutions that consistently teach and reward responsibility, fairness and contribution.

CHAPTER FOUR

POLITICAL LEADERSHIP: PUBLIC SERVICE OR PERSONAL ENRICHMENT?

4.1 Introduction

Political leadership is one of the most important determinants of the direction and pace of national development. Leaders influence the allocation of resources, the formulation of public policies, the management of institutions and the distribution of opportunities within society.

The distinction between givers and takers becomes particularly significant when examined within the context of political leadership.

A political leader may regard public office as an opportunity to serve society, improve the welfare of citizens and create conditions for sustainable development. Alternatively, public office may be regarded as an avenue for personal enrichment, family advancement, political patronage and the consolidation of private power.

These two orientations produce fundamentally different consequences for national development.

The giver-leader understands that political authority is a public trust. The taker-leader regards political authority as an instrument for acquiring benefits, privileges and influence.

For African countries, and particularly Nigeria, this distinction raises fundamental questions about the quality of political leadership, democratic accountability and the effective utilisation of national resources.

4.2 The Meaning of Political Giving

Political giving should not be confused with the distribution of money, food items or temporary material assistance to citizens.

True political giving involves creating the institutional, economic and social conditions that enable people to improve their lives.

A political leader gives when he or she:

Provides accessible and affordable education.

Promotes effective healthcare delivery.

Creates an enabling environment for employment and entrepreneurship.

Protects public resources against corruption and waste.

Provides essential infrastructure.

Promotes justice, equality and the rule of law.

Protects the rights and dignity of citizens.

Encourages innovation and productive investment.

Promotes national unity and social cohesion.

Develops institutions that can function effectively beyond the tenure of the incumbent administration.

Such contributions are not personal gifts from political officeholders. They are legitimate responsibilities of government.

Nevertheless, a leader who fulfils these responsibilities demonstrates the philosophy of giving through public service.

4.3 The Politics of Taking

Political taking occurs when individuals exploit public authority to secure personal or sectional advantages at the expense of the wider population.

It manifests in several ways:

Diversion of public funds.

Award of contracts without appropriate procedures.

Inflated contract costs.

Nepotistic appointments.

Political intimidation.

Misuse of security institutions.

Manipulation of public procurement.

Abuse of public property.

Diversion of development opportunities to political supporters.

Creation of unnecessary political positions for patronage.

The consequences extend beyond the immediate financial losses.

Public confidence declines, institutions become weakened, economic opportunities are distorted and citizens increasingly lose faith in democratic governance.

The tragedy is that countries possessing considerable natural and human resources may remain economically underdeveloped because the institutions responsible for managing those resources are captured by narrow private interests.

4.4 The African Leadership Challenge

Africa has experienced different forms of political leadership since independence.

Some leaders have contributed significantly to nation-building, educational expansion, infrastructure development and regional cooperation.

Others have presided over authoritarian systems, corruption, political instability and the concentration of economic opportunities in the hands of privileged groups.

The continent's development experience demonstrates that political independence does not automatically produce responsible governance.

The quality of leadership and the effectiveness of institutions remain essential.

A major challenge is the tendency to personalise political authority.

In some political environments, government programmes are presented as personal achievements of political officeholders rather than institutional responsibilities financed by taxpayers.

Roads, schools, hospitals and other public facilities are sometimes treated as political gifts.

This creates an unhealthy relationship between citizens and government.

Citizens should not have to demonstrate personal loyalty to political leaders before accessing public services to which they are legally entitled.

4.5 Nigeria: The Public Office Syndrome

Nigeria provides an important case study of the relationship between political authority and personal enrichment.

The country's political economy has historically been influenced by the importance of petroleum revenues, the centralisation of fiscal resources and the competition for control of government institutions.

In such circumstances, political office can become an attractive avenue for accessing and distributing economic opportunities.

The problem is not political ambition itself. Political ambition can motivate individuals to contribute to national development.

The problem arises when the desire to acquire political power becomes more important than the responsibilities associated with exercising it.

This produces what may be described as the public office syndrome: the tendency to regard public office as an opportunity for personal advancement rather than a position of public responsibility.

The consequences include:

Excessive political competition.

High electoral expenditure.

Patronage-based appointments.

Weak institutional accountability.

Public procurement irregularities.

Inefficient implementation of development programmes.

The diversion of attention from long-term national priorities.

The challenge is to transform political competition from a struggle for access to public resources into a competition of ideas, programmes, competence and developmental achievements.

4.6 Leadership and the Common Good

The concept of the common good provides an important framework for distinguishing responsible leadership from exploitative leadership.

The common good refers to the social, economic, political and institutional conditions that enable individuals and communities to achieve their legitimate aspirations.

It includes security, justice, education, healthcare, economic opportunities, social stability and respect for human dignity.

A giver-leader recognises that public policies should promote these collective interests.

A taker-leader may instead concentrate on policies that secure personal political survival or benefit particular groups.

This distinction is especially important in societies characterised by poverty and inequality.

Public resources are limited. Their allocation should therefore be guided by public needs, measurable benefits and fairness.

4.7 Leadership as Stewardship

The concept of stewardship provides a useful ethical foundation for political leadership.

A steward is entrusted with resources that belong to others and is expected to manage them responsibly.

Political leaders are therefore custodians of public resources rather than private owners of government institutions.

This understanding requires:

Transparency in public expenditure.

Accountability for development results.

Respect for constitutional limitations.

Responsible management of national assets.

Fairness in the distribution of opportunities.

Institutional continuity.

Respect for citizens' rights.

Political leadership should be judged not merely by speeches, promises and political popularity, but by demonstrable improvements in the welfare of citizens.

4.8 Conclusion

The difference between political giving and political taking is fundamental to the future of African development.

Countries require leaders who regard political authority as an instrument for improving human welfare.

Nigeria, in particular, needs a leadership culture that places public interest above private enrichment.

The ultimate test of political leadership is not the amount of wealth accumulated by political officeholders, but the quality of life enjoyed by the people they govern.

Central Lesson: Public office is a responsibility, not an inheritance or personal investment. The true political giver leaves behind stronger institutions, improved living conditions and better opportunities for future generations.

CHAPTER FIVE

THE POLITICS OF PATRONAGE, NEPOTISM AND STOMACH INFRASTRUCTURE IN NIGERIA

5.1 Introduction

One of the major manifestations of taking behaviour in Nigerian politics is the development of patronage relationships.

Political patronage involves the distribution of appointments, contracts, financial benefits and other opportunities in exchange for political support, loyalty or influence.

Although political patronage exists in different forms across many countries, it becomes particularly damaging when it replaces merit, fairness and institutional responsibility.

In Nigeria, the expression stomach infrastructure has become a popular description of a political culture in which immediate material benefits are used to secure political loyalty.

The phenomenon raises important questions about poverty, political dependency, democratic accountability and the quality of political representation.

5.2 Understanding Political Patronage

Patronage is a relationship in which a powerful individual or political organisation provides benefits to supporters in exchange for loyalty or assistance.

The relationship may involve:

Political appointments.

Distribution of money.

Award of government contracts.

Provision of scholarships.

Employment opportunities.

Access to public services.

Distribution of food and other relief materials.

Financial support for political mobilisation.

Not all political appointments or assistance programmes are necessarily patronage.

Governments legitimately appoint political advisers, provide social assistance and implement development programmes.

The distinction lies in the criteria governing the allocation of benefits.

When opportunities are distributed according to competence, established rules, public need and transparent procedures, they can contribute to development.

When they are distributed primarily according to personal loyalty, political allegiance or family connections, they become instruments of exclusion and dependency.

5.3 The Emergence of Stomach Infrastructure

The expression stomach infrastructure gained particular prominence in Nigerian political discourse during the 2014 governorship election in Ekiti State.

It was associated with the political importance of immediate material assistance to economically disadvantaged citizens.

The expression reflected a reality in which many citizens, confronted by poverty and economic insecurity, were more concerned with immediate survival than with long-term development promises.

For people who cannot afford daily meals, immediate food assistance may be genuinely valuable.

It would therefore be inappropriate to dismiss the needs of poor citizens or condemn every form of material assistance.

The real concern is the political exploitation of poverty.

When food distribution, small cash gifts and temporary assistance become substitutes for employment creation, education, healthcare, infrastructure and economic empowerment, the developmental implications become serious.

5.4 The Psychology of Political Dependency

Poverty can weaken the bargaining power of citizens.

A person who is unable to meet basic needs may become vulnerable to political inducements.

When political leaders distribute small amounts of money or food shortly before elections, some beneficiaries may feel compelled to support them.

The relationship can become one of dependency rather than accountability.

Instead of demanding effective public policies, citizens may become increasingly concerned with receiving immediate benefits from political patrons.

This creates a vicious circle:

Poverty weakens citizens' economic independence.

Economic vulnerability increases dependence on political patrons.

Political patrons use material benefits to secure loyalty.

Electoral loyalty may protect ineffective leadership.

Ineffective governance perpetuates poverty.

Continued poverty strengthens the patronage system.

Breaking this cycle requires economic empowerment, civic education and stronger democratic institutions.

5.5 Nepotism and the Distribution of Opportunities

Nepotism occurs when individuals favour relatives, friends or associates in appointments and other opportunities, irrespective of their comparative qualifications or suitability.

It is particularly harmful in public institutions because it undermines merit and fairness.

Consider a situation in which a qualified applicant is denied employment because a less qualified candidate has influential political connections.

The immediate consequence is individual injustice.

The wider consequence is institutional inefficiency.

When public appointments are based on personal relationships rather than competence, the quality of administration declines.

Nepotism can also create resentment, weaken organisational morale and discourage talented individuals from contributing to national development.

Nigeria's diversity makes the issue particularly sensitive.

Political leaders must ensure that appointments reflect constitutional provisions, fairness, competence and appropriate considerations of national inclusion.

Inclusion should not become an excuse for incompetence, just as merit should not become a disguise for sectional exclusion.

5.6 Patronage and the Electoral Process

Political patronage can influence electoral behaviour in several ways.

Candidates may distribute money or other material inducements to voters.

Political parties may use appointments and government contracts to reward loyal supporters.

Powerful individuals may mobilise political structures through financial relationships.

These practices can weaken democratic accountability.

The democratic process is intended to provide citizens with the opportunity to choose leaders based on competence, programmes, integrity and public performance.

When elections become primarily transactional, political representation is distorted.

The citizen becomes a beneficiary seeking immediate rewards rather than an active participant evaluating government performance.

5.7 The Cost to National Development

Patronage-based governance has several developmental consequences.

First, it weakens meritocracy. Qualified individuals may be excluded from positions where their abilities could improve public service delivery.

Second, it encourages corruption. Public contracts and appointments may become instruments for rewarding political supporters.

Third, it promotes inequality. Individuals with access to political power may enjoy opportunities unavailable to ordinary citizens.

Fourth, it weakens institutional independence. Public officials may become more accountable to political patrons than to the laws and rules governing their institutions.

Fifth, it encourages short-term thinking. Political leaders may prefer visible distributions of immediate benefits to investments in long-term development.

5.8 Replacing Political Dependency with Economic Empowerment

Nigeria requires a transition from patronage-based politics to productive empowerment.

This requires political leaders to focus on policies that expand citizens' capacity to generate sustainable incomes.

Important interventions include:

Quality basic and tertiary education.

Technical and vocational training.

Agricultural modernisation.

Access to affordable finance.

Support for small and medium-scale enterprises.

Industrial development.

Reliable electricity.

Improved transport infrastructure.

Digital entrepreneurship.

Transparent and inclusive social protection programmes.

Social assistance remains important, especially for vulnerable households.

However, it should be designed to protect human dignity, reduce vulnerability and, where appropriate, support pathways towards economic independence.

5.9 Conclusion

Political patronage, nepotism and stomach infrastructure demonstrate how the relationship between giving and taking can become distorted in democratic societies.

Material assistance is not inherently wrong. Indeed, it may be essential in societies where poverty and hunger are widespread.

The problem arises when assistance is used to manipulate citizens, secure political loyalty or conceal the failure to provide sustainable development opportunities.

Nigeria must move from a political system in which citizens are treated as dependants of powerful individuals towards one in which they are empowered as equal participants in national development.

Central Lesson: The greatest gift a political leader can give to citizens is not temporary relief but the opportunity to live productive, independent and dignified lives.

CHAPTER SIX

CORRUPTION, RESOURCE DIVERSION AND THE CULTURE OF TAKING

6.1 Introduction

Corruption represents one of the most destructive manifestations of taking behaviour in public and private institutions.

It occurs when entrusted authority is abused for private gain.

Corruption may involve bribery, embezzlement, fraud, extortion, nepotism, procurement manipulation, illegal enrichment and other forms of dishonest conduct.

Its consequences are particularly serious in developing countries where limited financial resources must support extensive social and economic development requirements.

For Nigeria and other African countries, corruption represents not merely a legal problem but also an ethical, institutional, economic and developmental challenge.

6.2 The Meaning and Nature of Corruption

Corruption is commonly associated with the abuse of entrusted power for private benefit.

However, its manifestations extend beyond the direct theft of public money.

It may occur when:

Public officials demand bribes before performing legitimate duties.

Government contracts are awarded through dishonest arrangements.

Public funds are diverted from approved purposes.

Recruitment processes are manipulated.

Government property is converted to private use.

Regulatory officials deliberately obstruct legitimate businesses.

Political officeholders use their influence to secure improper benefits.

Private businesses bribe officials to obtain unfair advantages.

Corruption can be individual or organised.

It can occur at different levels of government and across different sectors of the economy.

Its persistence often reflects institutional weaknesses, inadequate accountability and the normalisation of unethical practices.

6.3 Corruption as the Opposite of Giving

The relationship between giving and corruption is particularly important.

A public official who diverts resources intended for public hospitals is taking from people who depend on those facilities.

An official who manipulates educational contracts is taking opportunities away from children and future generations.

A public officer who demands bribes before providing legitimate services is exploiting citizens who should be treated fairly.

Corruption therefore involves more than the immediate financial loss.

It represents the denial of legitimate public benefits.

A single act of corruption may affect thousands of people.

For example, the diversion of funds intended for a rural water project may expose an entire community to preventable disease and unnecessary hardship.

The moral cost of corruption is therefore often greater than its recorded financial value.

6.4 The Resource Curse and African Development

The concept of the resource curse provides a useful framework for understanding why some countries rich in natural resources experience disappointing development outcomes.

Countries possessing petroleum, minerals and other valuable natural resources may face challenges associated with revenue volatility, institutional weaknesses, rent-seeking and excessive dependence on resource extraction.

Natural resource wealth does not automatically translate into broad-based prosperity.

The developmental outcome depends substantially on the institutions responsible for managing the resources.

Nigeria illustrates this challenge.

Petroleum has historically been a major source of government revenue and foreign exchange.

However, the country's development experience demonstrates that natural resource wealth must be converted into productive infrastructure, human capital, industrial capacity and diversified economic opportunities.

Where resource revenues are mismanaged, future generations may inherit environmental damage, inadequate infrastructure and weak economic institutions rather than sustainable prosperity.

6.5 The Political Economy of Taking

The political economy of taking examines how individuals and groups use political influence and institutional access to acquire economic advantages.

One manifestation is rent-seeking.

Rent-seeking occurs when individuals seek economic benefits through political influence, privileged access or manipulation of institutional arrangements rather than through productive economic activities.

Examples include:

Obtaining government contracts through improper influence.

Securing exclusive privileges without public justification.

Manipulating import or licensing arrangements.

Influencing regulations for private advantage.

Capturing public resources through political connections.

Such activities can divert entrepreneurial energy away from innovation and productive investment.

Instead of competing through better products, services and efficiency, individuals may compete for privileged access to government.

This is particularly damaging to economies seeking industrial transformation.

6.6 The Institutional Consequences of Corruption

Corruption undermines the effectiveness of institutions.

A public institution requires rules, procedures, professional standards and accountability mechanisms to perform its responsibilities.

When these arrangements are weakened by corruption, administrative performance deteriorates.

The consequences include:

Administrative inefficiency: Decisions may be influenced by bribery rather than established procedures.

Loss of public confidence: Citizens may begin to regard government institutions as untrustworthy.

Reduced investment: Investors may become discouraged by uncertainty, bribery and unfair competition.

Poor service delivery: Public resources may fail to reach their intended beneficiaries.

Social inequality: Individuals with money and influence may enjoy advantages unavailable to ordinary citizens.

Weak rule of law: The application of legal rules may become selective.

6.7 Corruption and the Nigerian Public Sector

Nigeria has established several institutions and legal frameworks to combat corruption.

Among the important institutions are:

Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC).

Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission (ICPC).

Code of Conduct Bureau.

Office of the Auditor-General for the Federation.

Public Accounts Committees of the National Assembly.

Bureau of Public Procurement.

These institutions perform different but complementary functions.

However, the existence of anti-corruption institutions does not automatically guarantee success.

Their effectiveness depends on independence, adequate capacity, professional competence, transparency and consistent application of the law.

Anti-corruption efforts must also extend beyond the investigation and prosecution of individual offenders.

They should address the institutional conditions that make corruption possible.

6.8 Public Financial Management as an Instrument for Combating Taking

Public Financial Management (PFM) is central to the prevention of corruption and the promotion of responsible resource utilisation.

A sound PFM system ensures that public resources are mobilised, allocated, spent, recorded and audited in accordance with approved procedures.

Important components include:

Credible budget preparation.

Legislative authorisation of expenditure.

Effective cash management.

Transparent procurement.

Reliable accounting systems.

Internal controls.

Independent external audit.

Legislative oversight.

Public access to budget information.

Monitoring and evaluation of development results.

The objective is to ensure that public resources produce measurable benefits.

The distinction between giving and taking becomes particularly visible in budget implementation.

A government may approve substantial allocations for healthcare, education and infrastructure.

However, the real developmental question is whether the money is spent for its intended purposes and whether citizens receive the expected benefits.

6.9 Beyond Punishment: Building Ethical Institutions

Punishment is important, but corruption cannot be eliminated through prosecution alone.

A comprehensive strategy must include:

Ethical leadership.

Effective internal controls.

Whistleblower protection.

Transparent procurement.

Digital financial management.

Public disclosure of relevant government information.

Independent audit.

Merit-based recruitment.

Civic education.

Consistent enforcement of the law.

Institutions should be designed in ways that reduce opportunities for dishonest behaviour.

At the same time, employees who demonstrate integrity should be protected, encouraged and appropriately rewarded.

6.10 Conclusion

Corruption is one of the clearest expressions of destructive taking behaviour.

It represents the appropriation of resources and opportunities that should contribute to collective welfare.

For Nigeria and other African countries, the fight against corruption must become an integral component of economic transformation and sustainable development.

The objective should not simply be to recover stolen money but to build institutions that protect public resources and ensure that every legitimate contribution produces meaningful results.

Central Lesson: Every amount diverted from public development represents opportunities denied to citizens. Responsible leadership requires protecting public resources as a trust held on behalf of present and future generations.

CHAPTER SEVEN

GIVERS AND TAKERS IN PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION AND INSTITUTIONAL GOVERNANCE

7.1 Introduction

Public administration provides the institutional machinery through which government policies are translated into programmes, projects and services.

It is responsible for implementing laws, managing public resources, coordinating development activities and delivering essential services.

The quality of public administration therefore has significant implications for national development.

Within this framework, the distinction between givers and takers can be examined through the attitudes and conduct of political officeholders, civil servants, public managers and other institutional actors.

A responsible public administrator regards public service as an opportunity to contribute to the welfare of citizens.

An irresponsible administrator may regard the same position as an avenue for personal enrichment, unnecessary privilege or the exercise of arbitrary power.

7.2 Public Administration as an Instrument of Giving

The fundamental purpose of public administration is to serve the public interest through the effective implementation of government policies.

Public administrators contribute to national development in several ways.

They prepare and implement budgets, coordinate government programmes, manage public institutions, supervise development projects and ensure the delivery of public services.

Their contributions may not always be visible to the general public, but they are essential to the functioning of government.

A competent budget officer who ensures that allocations reflect national priorities is a giver.

A procurement officer who protects public resources through transparent procedures is a giver.

A local government administrator who ensures that community development projects are properly implemented is a giver.

A public health administrator who coordinates effective healthcare delivery is a giver.

These examples demonstrate that giving in public administration is primarily about professional responsibility and the effective use of entrusted resources.

7.3 The Weberian Bureaucratic Tradition

Max Weber's analysis of bureaucracy provides an important foundation for understanding modern public administration.

The bureaucratic model emphasises hierarchy, defined responsibilities, technical competence, formal rules and impersonal administration.

These principles are intended to promote consistency, predictability and administrative efficiency.

A professional civil service should therefore operate according to established laws and procedures rather than the personal preferences of political officeholders.

The distinction between giving and taking becomes evident when administrators either uphold or undermine these principles.

A public administrator who follows transparent procedures contributes to institutional development.

An administrator who manipulates procedures for personal gain undermines the institution.

7.4 The Ethics of Public Service

Public service ethics refers to the moral principles and professional standards guiding the conduct of public officials.

These include:

Integrity.

Accountability.

Impartiality.

Transparency.

Professional competence.

Fairness.

Respect for the rule of law.

Responsiveness.

Stewardship.

Commitment to public interest.

These principles should influence decisions at every stage of public administration.

Public officials must recognise that public resources are not private property.

They are held in trust for the benefit of citizens.

7.5 The Nigerian Civil Service: Challenges and Opportunities

Nigeria's civil service has played an important role in the country's administrative and developmental history.

Its responsibilities include policy implementation, budget administration, personnel management, economic planning, regulation and service delivery.

The system has also experienced challenges involving political interference, inadequate capacity, procedural delays, weak accountability and corruption.

These problems should not obscure the contributions of professional public servants who have worked diligently under difficult circumstances.

The transformation of the Nigerian civil service requires renewed emphasis on:

Merit-based recruitment and promotion.

Continuous professional development.

Digitalisation of administrative processes.

Performance management.

Ethical leadership.

Effective supervision.

Improved working conditions.

Institutional accountability.

Policy continuity.

Results-oriented management.

The objective is to build a professional public service that is responsive to citizens and capable of supporting economic transformation.

7.6 Local Government Administration and Community Development

Local government is particularly important because it operates close to the people.

Its responsibilities may include primary healthcare support, local roads, sanitation, markets, community development and other services assigned by law.

A local government administrator who ensures that public resources produce visible community benefits demonstrates the philosophy of giving.

However, where local government resources are diverted or administrative processes are manipulated, communities suffer directly.

The consequences may include:

Abandoned projects.

Poor sanitation.

Inadequate rural roads.

Weak primary healthcare.

Poorly maintained markets.

Limited access to basic public services.

The quality of local government administration therefore has direct implications for grassroots development.

Community participation, financial transparency and effective supervision are essential.

7.7 Results-Based Management and Accountability

One of the most important approaches to strengthening public administration is results-based management.

This approach focuses on the relationship between resources, activities, outputs, outcomes and long-term impacts.

For example:

A government may allocate money to build primary healthcare centres.

The immediate output is the construction of facilities.

The expected outcome is improved access to healthcare.

The desired impact is better health and quality of life.

The existence of a completed building does not necessarily demonstrate successful service delivery.

The facility must be equipped, staffed, supplied and maintained.

Results-based management therefore requires administrators to focus on actual benefits rather than merely reporting expenditure or completed activities.

This approach is particularly relevant to the philosophy of giving because it emphasises the welfare improvements generated by public expenditure.

7.8 Public Financial Accountability

Accountability is central to the relationship between giving and taking in public administration.

Public officials should be answerable for decisions, expenditure and performance.

Effective accountability requires:

Clearly defined responsibilities.

Reliable financial records.

Regular internal audits.

Independent external audits.

Legislative oversight.

Performance evaluation.

Public access to relevant information.

Appropriate sanctions for misconduct.

Accountability should not be understood merely as punishment.

It also involves explaining decisions, demonstrating results and learning from administrative failures.

An accountable institution protects public resources and strengthens citizens' confidence.

7.9 The Role of Technology in Reducing Taking Behaviour

Digital technology offers important opportunities for improving administrative transparency.

Electronic procurement, digital payment systems, integrated financial management information systems, electronic personnel records and online service platforms can reduce opportunities for manipulation.

For example, electronic payment systems can reduce unnecessary human interference in the disbursement of government funds.

Digital procurement platforms can improve competition and provide records for subsequent audit.

However, technology is not a substitute for ethical leadership.

Digital systems can also be manipulated if access controls, cybersecurity arrangements and institutional oversight are inadequate.

Technology must therefore be supported by sound governance arrangements.

7.10 The Public Administrator as a Development Agent

Public administrators should be regarded as important agents of national development.

Their contribution goes beyond routine office procedures.

They help translate national aspirations into practical programmes and services.

They provide institutional continuity when political administrations change.

They also possess professional knowledge that can improve policy formulation and implementation.

A development-oriented public administrator understands that administrative efficiency must ultimately improve human welfare.

7.11 Conclusion

Public administration provides one of the most important institutional environments for translating the philosophy of giving into practical development outcomes.

The distinction between givers and takers is reflected in the way public officials manage authority, resources and responsibilities.

Nigeria requires a professional public service that places integrity, competence, accountability and measurable results at the centre of administrative practice.

Central Lesson: A good public administrator does not merely occupy an office. He or she creates public value, protects public resources and improves the quality of life of citizens.

CHAPTER EIGHT

THE PRIVATE SECTOR: WEALTH CREATION, EXPLOITATION AND CORPORATE SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY

8.1 Introduction

The private sector is an essential driver of economic growth and development.

Businesses create employment, produce goods and services, generate income, contribute to government revenue and promote technological innovation.

Entrepreneurs also identify opportunities, mobilise capital, assume risks and introduce new products and production methods.

However, the private sector can also become an arena in which the distinction between givers and takers is clearly demonstrated.

Some businesses create wealth while contributing to the welfare of workers, consumers and communities.

Others pursue profit through exploitation, dishonest practices, environmental degradation and the abuse of market power.

The central question is therefore not whether businesses should make profits, but how those profits are generated and whether business activities contribute positively to society.

8.2 Wealth Creation Versus Wealth Extraction

Wealth creation involves producing goods and services, developing markets, improving productivity and generating economic value.

Wealth extraction, in the negative sense used in this chapter, involves securing private benefits through exploitative practices that impose disproportionate costs on workers, consumers, communities or the wider economy.

A productive entrepreneur may establish a manufacturing business that employs hundreds of people, develops local supply chains and introduces innovative products.

An exploitative businessperson may manipulate prices, evade legitimate taxes, mistreat workers or use political connections to prevent fair competition.

Both may accumulate wealth, but their contributions to society are fundamentally different.

The distinction is important because economic growth should not be measured exclusively by private profits.

It should also consider employment quality, productivity, innovation, environmental sustainability and the distribution of economic opportunities.

8.3 The Entrepreneur as a Giver

Entrepreneurship can represent an important form of giving.

An entrepreneur who establishes a viable enterprise contributes capital, management skills, innovation and organisational capacity.

The resulting business may provide employment, improve consumer welfare and generate tax revenue.

Small and medium-scale enterprises are particularly important in African economies because they provide opportunities for self-employment and income generation.

In Nigeria, entrepreneurs operate in agriculture, manufacturing, transportation, construction, retail trade, information technology, professional services and other sectors.

Their contributions can be strengthened through:

Access to affordable finance.

Reliable electricity.

Improved transport infrastructure.

Appropriate taxation.

Business development services.

Technical education.

Research and innovation.

Stable regulatory arrangements.

Access to local and international markets.

The objective should be to create an environment in which productive businesses can grow and contribute to national prosperity.

8.4 Workers as Stakeholders in Wealth Creation

Employees are essential participants in business development.

They provide labour, knowledge, experience and organisational commitment.

A business cannot operate successfully without the contribution of its workforce.

Consequently, responsible employers should recognise employees as important stakeholders rather than merely as costs to be minimised.

A giving-oriented employer demonstrates responsibility through:

Fair and timely payment of wages.

Safe working conditions.

Respect for workers' dignity.

Opportunities for training and advancement.

Compliance with labour laws.

Non-discriminatory employment practices.

Appropriate social protection arrangements.

Constructive labour-management relations.

Exploitative employment practices can undermine productivity and create social tensions.

A business that accumulates substantial profits while denying workers fair treatment may be financially successful but ethically deficient.

8.5 Consumers and Business Ethics

Consumers are another important stakeholder group.

They depend on businesses for reliable products and services.

Responsible businesses should therefore provide accurate information, maintain appropriate quality standards and avoid misleading commercial practices.

Business ethics requires honesty in advertising, fair pricing, product safety and responsible treatment of customers.

In developing economies, where consumer protection mechanisms may face capacity challenges, ethical business conduct becomes particularly important.

The long-term success of a business depends substantially on consumer confidence.

Dishonesty may generate temporary financial advantages but can undermine reputation and sustainability.

8.6 Corporate Social Responsibility

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) refers broadly to the responsibility of businesses to consider their social, environmental and economic impacts.

It recognises that business organisations operate within communities and depend on social and environmental systems.

Corporate responsibility may include:

Educational support.

Healthcare programmes.

Environmental protection.

Skills acquisition.

Community infrastructure.

Youth entrepreneurship.

Support for vulnerable groups.

Responsible waste management.

Local employment.

Ethical supply-chain practices.

In Nigeria, CSR has become particularly relevant in sectors such as petroleum, telecommunications, banking, manufacturing and extractive industries.

However, CSR should not become a substitute for responsible business operations.

A company cannot reasonably claim to be socially responsible simply because it donates money to a school while maintaining exploitative labour practices or causing avoidable environmental damage.

Responsible business conduct must be reflected in the company's everyday operations.

8.7 The Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry: A Case Study

Nigeria's petroleum industry provides an important example of the complex relationship between wealth creation and social responsibility.

The industry has generated substantial government revenue, foreign exchange earnings, employment and business opportunities.

At the same time, petroleum exploration and production have raised concerns about environmental degradation, community relations, resource management and the distribution of economic benefits.

Communities in oil-producing areas have frequently expressed concerns about pollution, damage to livelihoods, inadequate development opportunities and the responsibilities of companies operating in their environments.

These concerns demonstrate that resource extraction must be accompanied by effective environmental management, transparent regulation and responsible community engagement.

A responsible petroleum enterprise should consider not only the commercial value of its operations but also their long-term implications for communities and the environment.

The broader lesson applies to mining, agriculture, construction and other sectors.

Economic activities should not undermine the conditions necessary for sustainable human development.

8.8 Philanthropy and the Limits of Corporate Giving

Business philanthropy can make important contributions to society.

Successful entrepreneurs may establish foundations, finance scholarships, support hospitals and provide assistance to disadvantaged communities.

Such contributions deserve recognition.

However, philanthropy should not replace the responsibilities of government or the obligations of businesses to operate ethically.

A wealthy entrepreneur who funds educational programmes contributes positively to society.

Nevertheless, the contribution does not remove the need to pay legitimate taxes, respect workers' rights and comply with environmental regulations.

Similarly, government should not abandon its responsibility to provide public education and healthcare merely because private philanthropists are willing to assist.

Sustainable development requires cooperation between government, business and civil society.

8.9 Building a Responsible Private Sector in Nigeria

Nigeria requires a private sector that combines profitability with productive contribution.

This involves strengthening the relationship between entrepreneurship, innovation, employment creation and social responsibility.

Important policy directions include:

Improving the business environment.

Strengthening competition and consumer protection.

Promoting local manufacturing.

Supporting small and medium-scale enterprises.

Expanding technical and vocational training.

Encouraging responsible investment.

Improving access to finance.

Strengthening environmental regulation.

Promoting corporate transparency.

Encouraging public-private partnerships with clear accountability.

Public-private partnerships should be structured to produce genuine public value.

They should not become mechanisms for transferring public resources into private hands without corresponding benefits to society.

8.10 Conclusion

The private sector can be a powerful instrument for transforming Nigeria and other African economies.

Businesses that create employment, introduce innovation, pay legitimate taxes and respect workers and consumers demonstrate the positive relationship between wealth creation and social contribution.

However, private wealth accumulation through exploitation and unfair advantage represents a form of destructive taking.

The objective should be to develop a business culture in which profitability and social responsibility reinforce one another.

Central Lesson: The best business is not simply one that makes money. It is one that creates sustainable value, treats people fairly and contributes to the prosperity of the society in which it operates.

RELIGION, TRADITIONAL INSTITUTIONS AND THE MORAL RESPONSIBILITY OF LEADERSHIP

Religion and traditional institutions occupy important positions in African societies. They influence individual behaviour, family relationships, community organisation, cultural identity and social values. They also contribute to the development of moral standards through teachings about honesty, compassion, generosity, justice, responsibility and respect for human dignity.

Across Africa, religious institutions have historically provided education, healthcare, humanitarian assistance and support for disadvantaged communities. Traditional institutions have equally played important roles in conflict resolution, community mobilisation, cultural preservation and the maintenance of social cohesion.

These institutions are therefore strategically positioned to promote a culture of giving and discourage exploitative forms of taking.

However, religious and traditional institutions are not automatically immune from the problems they seek to address. Their leaders may also become involved in materialism, excessive personal enrichment, abuse of authority, discrimination and the manipulation of followers.

The central question is whether religious and traditional leadership can consistently demonstrate the values of service, integrity and responsibility that they encourage others to practise.

9.2 Religion as an Instrument of Moral Development

Religion provides moral and ethical teachings that influence the behaviour of individuals and communities.

Christianity, Islam and African traditional religions, despite their theological differences, contain teachings that emphasise responsibilities towards other people.

Christianity encourages believers to demonstrate love, compassion, forgiveness, generosity and concern for the poor.

Islam emphasises charity, justice, compassion and the responsibility of individuals to assist disadvantaged members of society.

African traditional religious and cultural systems have historically emphasised respect for elders, communal obligations, social harmony and the importance of maintaining good relationships within families and communities.

These teachings provide important foundations for developing a society in which people recognise responsibilities beyond their immediate personal interests.

POVERTY, INEQUALITY AND THE DISTRIBUTION OF OPPORTUNITIES IN AFRICAN SOCIETIES

10.1 Introduction

Poverty and inequality remain among the most significant development challenges confronting many African countries.

Although Africa possesses considerable natural resources, agricultural potential, human capital and entrepreneurial opportunities, the benefits of economic activity are not always distributed equitably.

Millions of people continue to experience difficulties accessing quality education, healthcare, decent employment, housing, electricity, clean water and other essential services.

The relationship between givers and takers provides an important framework for examining these challenges.

A society in which opportunities are monopolised by privileged groups may experience persistent inequality, social exclusion and economic stagnation.

Conversely, a society that encourages productive contribution, equitable opportunities and responsible resource distribution creates better conditions for inclusive development.

10.2 Understanding Poverty

Poverty is a multidimensional condition involving inadequate access to the resources and opportunities required for a dignified standard of living.

It is not simply the absence of money.

Poverty may involve:

- Inadequate food and nutrition.

- Limited educational opportunities.

- Poor healthcare.

- Unemployment.

- Inadequate housing.

- Lack of clean water.

- Limited access to electricity.

- Social exclusion.

- Vulnerability to economic shocks.

- Inability to participate effectively in social and economic activities.

The multidimensional nature of poverty suggests that its reduction requires more than temporary financial assistance.

It requires investment in human capabilities, infrastructure, institutions and productive economic opportunities.

10.3 The Relationship Between Giving and Poverty Reduction

Giving can contribute significantly to poverty reduction.

Individuals, organisations and governments can support disadvantaged populations through education, healthcare, employment opportunities, social protection and productive investment.

However, the nature of giving matters.

There is a difference between giving someone money to meet an immediate need and providing the knowledge, skills, resources and opportunities that enable that person to become economically independent.

Both forms of assistance may be necessary.

A household facing hunger requires immediate relief.

A young person who has completed secondary education may require vocational training, financial assistance or access to employment.

A rural community may require roads, irrigation facilities, electricity and agricultural extension services.

Effective development policy must recognise these different needs.

10.4 Inequality and the Concentration of Wealth

Economic inequality refers to differences in the distribution of income, wealth and economic opportunities.

Some degree of income difference may arise from variations in skills, productivity, investment and entrepreneurial success.

However, inequality becomes particularly problematic when opportunities are determined primarily by inherited privilege, political connections, discrimination or unequal access to essential services.

When a small group controls disproportionate economic opportunities while large sections of society remain excluded, the foundations of social cohesion may weaken.

The consequences include:

1. Social resentment.

2. Limited social mobility.

3. Reduced access to education.

4. Unemployment and underemployment.

5. Political instability.

6. Intergenerational poverty.

7. Unequal access to justice.

8. Reduced confidence in public institutions.

The objective should not be to discourage wealth creation.

Rather, it should be to ensure that economic opportunities are sufficiently inclusive and that wealth accumulation contributes to broader social progress.

10.5 Nigeria: The Contradiction Between Wealth and Poverty

Nigeria presents an important example of the development paradox associated with resource abundance and widespread socioeconomic difficulties.

The country possesses substantial petroleum resources, agricultural potential, a large domestic market, a youthful population and a vibrant entrepreneurial sector.

Nevertheless, many households continue to experience serious economic insecurity.

This contradiction raises important questions about the management of national resources and the effectiveness of development institutions.

Why should a country with considerable natural and human resources struggle to provide adequate infrastructure and essential services to all its citizens?

Why do educational opportunities remain unequal?

Why do many young people experience difficulties obtaining productive employment?

Why do rural communities frequently experience inadequate access to basic services?

These questions cannot be answered through explanations based exclusively on the shortage of resources.

They also require examining the quality of governance, economic structure, institutional capacity, public expenditure and the distribution of opportunities.

10.6 The Difference Between Charity and Empowerment

Charity and empowerment are complementary but different approaches to addressing poverty.

Charity provides assistance to individuals experiencing hardship.

Empowerment strengthens their ability to improve their circumstances through skills, resources, knowledge and opportunities.

For example, providing food to a hungry household is an important humanitarian intervention.

Helping the household acquire productive skills, access employment or establish a viable enterprise may provide a longer-term pathway out of poverty.

Similarly, distributing educational materials to disadvantaged children is useful.

Ensuring that they receive quality teaching, adequate facilities and opportunities for further education is even more transformative.

The best development programmes combine immediate assistance with long-term empowerment.

10.7 Social Protection and the Responsibility of Government

Social protection refers to public policies and programmes designed to reduce poverty, vulnerability and social exclusion.

These may include:

- Cash transfer programmes.

- Social insurance.

- Pensions.

- Public employment programmes.

- Disability support.

- School feeding.

- Maternal and child health interventions.

- Emergency assistance.

- Unemployment support where established.

Social protection should not be regarded as an expression of government generosity.

It is an important instrument of social policy and economic stability.

The responsibility of government is to ensure that vulnerable citizens receive appropriate protection while promoting opportunities for productive participation in the economy.

Programmes must be transparent, properly targeted, monitored and evaluated.

10.8 Rural-Urban Inequality

Development opportunities are not always evenly distributed between rural and urban areas.

Urban centres may enjoy better access to educational institutions, hospitals, financial services, transportation and employment opportunities.

Rural communities may experience inadequate infrastructure and limited access to essential services.

This imbalance encourages migration towards cities, sometimes placing additional pressure on urban infrastructure.

Nigeria must therefore promote balanced regional development.

Important interventions include:

1. Rural road construction.

2. Agricultural modernisation.

3. Irrigation development.

4. Rural electrification.

5. Primary healthcare.

6. Quality rural education.

7. Digital connectivity.

8. Agro-processing facilities.

9. Access to agricultural finance.

10. Development of rural markets.

These interventions can transform rural communities from areas of economic disadvantage into productive centres of economic activity.

10.9 Intergenerational Poverty and the Responsibility to Future Generations

Poverty can be transmitted from one generation to another.

Children born into disadvantaged households may have limited access to quality education, healthcare and nutrition.

These disadvantages can affect their future employment opportunities and earning capacity.

The result is a cycle in which poverty becomes intergenerational.

Breaking this cycle requires substantial investment in children and young people.

Education, nutrition, preventive healthcare, early childhood development and social protection are essential.

A society that neglects its children is effectively taking opportunities away from future generations.

A society that invests in them demonstrates a commitment to sustainable development.

10.10 The Role of Wealthy Individuals and Philanthropy

Wealthy individuals can contribute to poverty reduction through philanthropy, entrepreneurship, investment and community development.

Their contributions may include scholarships, hospitals, research funding, vocational training and support for small businesses.

However, philanthropy should be guided by measurable social impact and transparency.

It should also complement responsible public policy.

The responsibility for addressing structural poverty cannot be transferred entirely to private donors.

Government must establish the institutional conditions required for equitable access to opportunities.

10.11 Conclusion

Poverty and inequality are not merely economic problems. They are also questions of social justice, institutional responsibility and the distribution of opportunities.

The philosophy of giving requires societies to recognise the dignity and potential of every individual.

For Nigeria and other African countries, the challenge is to move beyond temporary assistance towards policies that expand human capabilities, promote productive employment and reduce structural inequalities.

Central Lesson: The greatest contribution to poverty reduction is not merely giving people something to consume, but creating conditions in which they can produce, earn, participate and live with dignity.

GIVERS AND TAKERS IN POLITICS, DEMOCRACY AND NATIONAL DEVELOPMENT

13.1 Introduction

Democracy is based on the principle that political authority derives from the people and should be exercised in accordance with constitutional provisions and the public interest.

It provides opportunities for citizens to participate in the selection of leaders, influence public policies and hold government accountable.

However, the existence of democratic institutions does not automatically guarantee responsible leadership or sustainable development.

Democratic systems can be undermined when political actors regard public authority as an avenue for personal enrichment rather than an opportunity to serve society.

The distinction between givers and takers is therefore central to understanding the quality of democracy and its developmental outcomes.

13.2 Democracy and the Public Interest

Democracy should promote political participation, accountability, equality before the law and respect for human rights.

Its legitimacy depends substantially on the extent to which institutions protect citizens and respond to their needs.

A democratic leader demonstrates giving behaviour by:

1. Respecting constitutional limitations.

2. Promoting the rule of law.

3. Protecting fundamental rights.

4. Providing essential public services.

5. Encouraging public participation.

6. Promoting national unity.

7. Managing public resources responsibly.

8. Supporting independent institutions.

9. Accepting legitimate criticism.

10. Ensuring peaceful political succession.

A leader who uses democratic office to weaken institutions and concentrate personal power undermines the very foundations of democratic governance.

13.3 Elections: Service Mandate or Investment Opportunity?

Elections provide an opportunity for citizens to choose representatives and determine the direction of government.

However, the electoral process can become distorted when political office is treated as a financial investment.

In such circumstances, candidates may spend excessive resources seeking political power with the expectation of recovering their investments through access to government contracts, appointments and public resources.

This creates a dangerous relationship between political financing and public accountability.

The problem is not that political campaigns require resources.

The problem is when the pursuit of political office becomes dominated by expectations of private financial returns.

The democratic process should encourage candidates to compete through credible programmes, competence, integrity and public service records.

13.4 Political Parties and Internal Democracy

Political parties are important institutions in democratic systems.

They recruit political leaders, formulate policies, organise electoral campaigns and provide platforms for political participation.

Their internal organisation has implications for the quality of national leadership.

Where political parties are controlled by a small group of powerful individuals, candidate selection may become influenced by personal relationships, financial contributions and patronage.

This may exclude competent individuals and weaken public confidence.

Political parties should therefore promote:

- Transparent candidate selection.

- Internal accountability.

- Democratic participation.

- Policy development.

- Financial transparency.

- Gender inclusion.

- Youth participation.

- Ethical political conduct.

A political party that promotes internal democracy contributes to national democratic development.

13.5 Opposition Politics and National Responsibility

The opposition performs an important function in democratic governance.

It provides alternative policy proposals, examines government performance and holds the ruling party accountable.

However, opposition politics should not be reduced to the deliberate obstruction of every government initiative.

Responsible opposition requires constructive criticism and the presentation of credible alternatives.

Similarly, the ruling party should recognise that political opponents are legitimate participants in the democratic process.

The national interest should be placed above narrow partisan considerations.

Both government and opposition can demonstrate the philosophy of giving by contributing to the improvement of democratic institutions.

13.6 Federalism, Resource Distribution and Political Competition in Nigeria

Nigeria operates a federal system of government involving the federal, state and local government levels.

The distribution of constitutional responsibilities and financial resources among these levels has significant implications for national development.

Federalism is intended to accommodate diversity, promote effective administration and provide opportunities for different levels of government to address local needs.

However, competition for control of financial resources and political influence can create tensions.

A responsible federal system requires:

- Clearly defined constitutional responsibilities.

- Fair and transparent revenue allocation.

- Effective intergovernmental relations.

- Fiscal accountability.

- Respect for subnational autonomy within constitutional limits.

- Coordination of national development priorities.

- Appropriate local participation.

The philosophy of giving requires every level of government to use its resources to improve citizens' welfare.

The objective should not be merely to control the largest share of public revenue, but to ensure that available resources produce measurable development outcomes.

13.7 Political Inclusion and National Unity

Nigeria is a diverse country with different ethnic, linguistic, religious and cultural communities.

This diversity provides opportunities for national enrichment but also creates challenges involving representation, political competition and social cohesion.

Political leaders should recognise that national development requires cooperation across sectional boundaries.

Appointments, resource allocation and public policies should be guided by constitutional provisions, competence, fairness and national interest.

The manipulation of ethnic or religious differences for political advantage represents a destructive form of taking because it sacrifices collective welfare for narrow political benefits.

A giver-oriented political system promotes national unity and recognises the dignity and rights of all citizens.

Democracy requires responsible citizens as well as responsible leaders.

7. Monitoring government programmes.

8. Participating in community development.

9. Respecting the rights of others.

10. Demanding transparent governance.

Civil society organisations and independent media play important roles in promoting accountability.

They investigate public policies, educate citizens, monitor government programmes and provide platforms for public debate.

Their contributions help strengthen transparency and democratic participation.

Information should be accurate, balanced and responsibly communicated.

Organisations that manipulate public opinion for private interests may weaken democratic trust.

The philosophy of giving requires these institutions to contribute to informed citizenship and constructive public engagement.

The concept of the developmental state provides another useful perspective.

A developmental state is one in which public institutions possess the capacity and commitment to promote economic transformation.

Such a state invests in infrastructure, education, industrial development, research, technology and institutional capacity.

13.12 Conclusion

Democracy provides opportunities for responsible leadership, but its success depends on the values and behaviour of political actors and citizens.

The distinction between givers and takers helps explain why some democratic systems produce meaningful development while others struggle with corruption, patronage and institutional weakness.

Nigeria requires a democratic culture in which political authority is treated as a public trust.

Political leaders should be evaluated by their contributions to national welfare, while citizens must exercise their democratic responsibilities with independence and integrity.

Central Lesson: The success of democracy should not be measured merely by the frequency of elections, but by the quality of governance, the protection of citizens' rights and the improvements achieved in their lives.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN

BUILDING A CULTURE OF GIVING: INTEGRITY, ACCOUNTABILITY AND THE COMMON GOOD

14.1 Introduction

The development challenges confronting African countries cannot be adequately addressed through economic policies, institutional reforms and political restructuring alone. Although these measures are essential, sustainable development also requires a transformation of the values, attitudes and behavioural patterns that influence how individuals, organisations and governments relate to one another.

The distinction between givers and takers provides an important framework for understanding this challenge.

A giving society is one in which individuals and institutions recognise that their existence and prosperity are interconnected with the welfare of others. It is a society where people appreciate that personal success becomes more meaningful when it contributes positively to the lives of other people.

Conversely, a taking society is characterised by excessive self-interest, exploitation, corruption, irresponsibility and the pursuit of personal advantages without adequate consideration for the consequences on society.

Africa's development requires a deliberate transition from excessive individualism and institutional exploitation towards a culture of responsible contribution, accountability and shared prosperity.

This transformation must begin with individuals but must ultimately become embedded in families, educational institutions, religious organisations, businesses, political parties and government institutions.

14.2 Understanding the Culture of Giving

A culture of giving refers to a social environment in which individuals and institutions consistently demonstrate willingness to contribute towards the welfare and development of others.

Giving should not be understood exclusively in financial terms.

It encompasses the contribution of:

Knowledge and professional expertise.

Time and personal commitment.

Financial and material resources.

Mentorship and guidance.

Emotional and social support.

Leadership and institutional capacity.

Innovation and entrepreneurship.

Community service.

Voluntary participation.

Responsible management of public resources.

A lecturer who dedicates time to improving students' knowledge is a giver.

A public administrator who ensures that public funds are used for their intended purposes is a giver.

An entrepreneur who creates employment and treats workers fairly is a giver.

A political leader who prioritises public welfare over personal enrichment is a giver.

A parent who invests in the education and character development of children is a giver.

These examples demonstrate that giving is a broad social responsibility rather than an activity restricted to philanthropy.

14.3 Integrity as the Foundation of Giving

Integrity is one of the most important characteristics of a giving society.

It involves consistency between personal values, expressed commitments and actual behaviour.

An individual who professes honesty but engages in deception cannot be regarded as demonstrating integrity.

Similarly, a political leader who promises development but deliberately diverts public resources for personal benefit contradicts the principles of responsible leadership.

Integrity requires:

Honesty in personal and professional relationships.

Respect for the rights of others.

Transparency in financial transactions.

Responsible use of authority.

Fulfilment of legitimate obligations.

Accountability for decisions.

Opposition to corruption.

Respect for established laws and ethical standards.

Integrity is particularly important in public administration because government officials exercise authority over resources belonging to the public.

A public officer should understand that public office is a position of trust, not an opportunity for personal enrichment.

14.4 Accountability and Responsible Resource Management

Accountability refers to the obligation of individuals and institutions to explain and justify their decisions and actions and accept responsibility for their consequences.

It is an essential requirement for building public confidence.

In Nigeria, accountability should operate at all levels of government.

Federal, state and local government institutions should demonstrate transparency in budgeting, procurement, expenditure management and programme implementation.

The public should be able to understand how government resources are generated, allocated and utilised.

Accountability must also extend to private organisations, religious institutions, charitable foundations and community associations.

Organisations that receive financial contributions from members or donors have a responsibility to demonstrate that those resources are properly managed.

Giving without accountability can create opportunities for exploitation.

14.5 The Common Good as a Development Philosophy

The common good refers to the social conditions that enable individuals and communities to achieve their legitimate objectives and enjoy a dignified quality of life.

It recognises that human welfare is interconnected.

The construction of a good road benefits individual motorists, businesses, farmers, students and the wider community.

An effective public healthcare system benefits not only patients but also employers, families and the national economy.

Quality education contributes to individual advancement while strengthening national productivity.

The common good therefore requires policies and institutions that promote shared opportunities.

It does not mean suppressing individual ambition or discouraging private wealth creation.

Rather, it means ensuring that personal advancement does not depend on the deliberate exploitation of others.

Educational institutions are particularly important in developing the values required for a giving society.

Discipline.

Compassion.

Tolerance.

Cooperation.

Civic responsibility.

Environmental consciousness.

Social justice.

Respect for public property.

Students should be encouraged to participate in community development activities.

Universities can promote research directed towards solving practical national problems.

Professional institutions should encourage ethical conduct among their members.

Education becomes more meaningful when knowledge is applied to improving human welfare.

14.7 Leadership by Example

The development of a giving society requires leaders who demonstrate the values they expect others to practise.

Leadership by example is more influential than the mere delivery of speeches.

Parents who demonstrate honesty influence their children.

Political leaders who demonstrate accountability strengthen democratic institutions.

14.8 Corporate Social Responsibility and Ethical Business

Philanthropy: Voluntary contributions of resources to support humanitarian, educational, social or developmental objectives.

Public Financial Management: The systems and processes through which public resources are planned, allocated, utilised, controlled and accounted for.

Public Trust: The responsibility associated with exercising authority or managing resources on behalf of the public.

Self-Interest: The pursuit of one's own welfare or objectives.

Social Capital: Networks, relationships, trust and shared values that facilitate cooperation.

Social Protection: Policies and programmes designed to reduce poverty, vulnerability and social exclusion.

Sustainable Development: Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Takers: In the negative behavioural sense used in this book, individuals or institutions that pursue personal advantages through exploitation, manipulation or irresponsible appropriation of resources.

9. FINAL AUTHOR'S MESSAGE

Africa's development requires a change in the way individuals, institutions and governments understand success.

Success should not be measured exclusively by the wealth accumulated, political authority acquired, positions occupied or influence exercised.

It should also be measured by the knowledge shared, opportunities created, institutions strengthened, lives improved and contributions made to the common good.

Nigeria and other African countries need more responsible wealth creators, ethical political leaders, accountable public administrators, committed professionals and citizens who recognise their obligations to society.

The philosophy of giving should therefore become an integral part of African development thinking and practice.

Let us build a society in which giving is celebrated, integrity is rewarded, accountability is enforced, productive enterprise is encouraged and human dignity is protected.

For the giver may not always accumulate the greatest material wealth, but a society that encourages responsible giving stands a better chance of achieving sustainable prosperity.
