CHAPTER ONE

UNDERSTANDING SERENITY AND TRANQUILLITY

1.1 Introduction

Human beings have always searched for peace, security, happiness and a sense of fulfilment. From the earliest communities to the complex societies of the twenty-first century, people have sought conditions in which they can live without unnecessary fear, hostility and disruption. Yet the search for peace has often been approached primarily from the perspective of preventing wars, suppressing violence or maintaining political order. While these objectives are important, peace has a much deeper foundation.

A peaceful society ultimately depends upon the quality of the individuals and relationships that constitute it.

This observation provides the starting point for understanding serenity and tranquillity. Serenity is commonly associated with inner calm, composure, emotional balance and freedom from unnecessary agitation. Tranquillity, on the other hand, is generally associated with peacefulness, quietness, stability and the absence of serious disturbance. Although the two concepts overlap, they are not identical. Serenity may begin within the individual, while tranquillity may describe the peaceful condition of relationships, communities and societies.

The distinction is important for development.

A country may experience an absence of open warfare and yet remain deeply troubled by poverty, injustice, corruption, insecurity, discrimination, unemployment, social exclusion and institutional distrust. Such a country may be formally at peace but lack the deeper conditions necessary for tranquillity and sustainable development.

Similarly, an individual may possess material wealth but experience considerable internal turmoil. Consequently, material prosperity alone cannot be regarded as the complete measure of human wellbeing.

This chapter therefore examines the meaning and significance of serenity and tranquillity and establishes the conceptual foundation upon which the subsequent chapters of this book will be developed.

1.2 The Meaning of Serenity

Serenity refers broadly to a state of calmness, composure and inner balance. It is the capacity of an individual to remain reasonably settled in thought and emotion despite the challenges and uncertainties of life.

Serenity should not be confused with the absence of problems. Life inevitably contains disappointment, uncertainty, loss, disagreement, competition and change. A serene person is not necessarily someone who has no difficulties. Rather, serenity is reflected in the manner in which difficulties are confronted.

The serene individual is capable of pausing before reacting, thinking before deciding and considering consequences before acting. Such a person is less likely to allow temporary anger, fear or frustration to determine important decisions.

Serenity therefore has an important behavioural dimension.

It encourages:

  • emotional self-control; - patience; - tolerance; - thoughtful decision-making; - responsible communication; - resilience; - empathy; - forgiveness; - moderation; and - constructive responses to adversity.

In this sense, serenity represents an important form of personal capital. An individual who can manage emotions and respond rationally to challenges is better positioned to maintain productive relationships and contribute positively to society.

Tranquillity extends beyond the individual to the surrounding social environment.

A tranquil family is one in which disagreements can be managed without persistent hostility. A tranquil community is one in which people of different backgrounds can coexist peacefully. A tranquil nation is one in which political competition, social differences and economic interests are managed through legitimate institutions rather than violence and intimidation.

Tranquillity therefore has both social and institutional dimensions.

It depends upon such conditions as:

1. personal responsibility; 2. mutual respect; 3. justice; 4. security; 5. trust; 6. inclusion; 7. effective institutions; 8. accountability; 9. economic opportunity; and 10. peaceful mechanisms for resolving disagreements.

A community cannot remain tranquil for long if large sections of its population feel permanently excluded or unjustly treated. Similarly, a nation cannot sustain tranquillity where citizens have little confidence in the fairness of public institutions.

Thus, tranquillity is not merely silence. A society in which people are afraid to express legitimate opinions may appear quiet but cannot necessarily be described as genuinely tranquil.

True tranquillity is compatible with freedom, justice, participation and constructive disagreement.

1.3 Serenity and Tranquillity: Similarities and Differences

Although the concepts are closely related, their principal areas of application differ.

Serenity| Tranquillity Primarily begins within the individual| Extends strongly into relationships and society Concerned with inner calm| Concerned with peaceful coexistence Emphasises emotional balance| Emphasises social stability Encourages thoughtful responses| Encourages constructive relationships Supports personal wellbeing| Supports community and national wellbeing Strengthens individual resilience| Strengthens collective resilience

The two concepts are nevertheless mutually reinforcing.

A society composed largely of emotionally unstable, intolerant and irresponsible individuals will find it difficult to maintain tranquillity. Conversely, an individual living continually in an environment characterised by violence, insecurity and injustice may find it difficult to maintain serenity.

This produces a reciprocal relationship:

Serene individuals → healthy relationships → tranquil communities → stable nations → peaceful international relations.

The reverse can also occur:

Social disorder → insecurity → anxiety and distrust → damaged relationships → reduced personal serenity.

The objective of development should therefore be to strengthen both personal and collective conditions of peace.

1.4 Serenity Is Not Passivity

One possible misunderstanding must be addressed.

Serenity does not mean passivity, weakness or indifference.

A serene person can be courageous. A tranquil society can be politically active. Indeed, some of the most important struggles for justice have been led by individuals who demonstrated extraordinary composure in confronting injustice.

Serenity means that action is guided by reason, values and purpose rather than uncontrolled emotion.

A citizen may strongly oppose corruption while remaining peaceful. A community may demand justice without resorting to violence. A government may defend national interests while maintaining diplomatic relations with other countries.

This distinction is especially important in democratic societies.

Constructive disagreement is not the opposite of tranquillity. Rather, the ability to disagree without destroying relationships or institutions is one of the highest expressions of tranquillity.

Development has traditionally been measured using indicators such as income, employment, education, health, infrastructure and economic growth. These indicators remain important. However, development ultimately concerns human beings.

The question should therefore not only be:

How much does a society produce?

It should also be:

How well do its people live?

Human development requires opportunities for people to lead lives they have reason to value. Such lives require more than income. They require health, education, security, dignity, meaningful relationships, participation and freedom from unnecessary fear.

Serenity contributes to these objectives by strengthening an individual's capacity to make responsible choices.

A person who is constantly overwhelmed by anger, fear or insecurity may struggle to study effectively, work productively, maintain healthy relationships or contribute constructively to the community.

At the collective level, tranquillity creates an environment in which economic and social activities can flourish.

Businesses require security. Schools require stability. Hospitals require functioning institutions. Investors require predictability. Families require safety. Communities require trust.

Consequently, peace is not simply a consequence of development; it is also a condition that enables development.

1.5 Serenity, Tranquillity and Sustainable Development

Sustainable development is commonly understood as development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

This principle has traditionally been associated with three broad dimensions:

  • economic development; - social development; and - environmental sustainability.

Serenity and tranquillity introduce a further human dimension: the quality of relationships through which development takes place.

Economic growth accompanied by severe social conflict cannot easily be sustained. Environmental policies imposed without consultation may generate resistance. Development projects that exclude local populations can create distrust. Economic inequality can undermine social cohesion.

Sustainability therefore requires cooperation.

Cooperation requires trust.

Trust requires responsible behaviour.

Responsible behaviour is strengthened by values such as patience, fairness, empathy, moderation and respect.

In this way, serenity and tranquillity are indirectly connected to sustainability.

1.6 The Nigerian Context

The relevance of serenity and tranquillity is particularly significant in Nigeria.

Nigeria is a country of remarkable ethnic, linguistic, religious, cultural and socioeconomic diversity. Its diversity is potentially one of its greatest strengths. However, when differences are manipulated for political, economic or social advantage, diversity can become a source of tension.

Nigeria has also experienced challenges involving insecurity, corruption, unemployment, poverty, inequality, political competition, communal disputes and environmental degradation.

These challenges cannot be addressed solely through coercive measures.

They require:

  • responsible leadership; - justice and fairness; - credible institutions; - respect for diversity; - effective citizenship; - economic opportunity; - social inclusion; - dialogue; - accountability; and - a culture of peaceful conflict resolution.

Nigeria's development challenge is therefore partly a challenge of building institutions and social relationships capable of accommodating differences peacefully.

The concept of tranquillity does not imply that Nigerians should suppress legitimate grievances. Rather, it requires the creation of mechanisms through which grievances can be expressed and addressed without unnecessary violence or destruction.

Across Africa, many societies have made significant progress in democracy, education, economic development, technology and regional cooperation. Nevertheless, the continent continues to confront challenges including poverty, inequality, political instability, insecurity, unemployment, displacement and environmental pressures.

These challenges demonstrate that sustainable development requires more than economic resources.

Africa possesses considerable natural and human resources. What is frequently required is the strengthening of institutions and relationships that enable these resources to be converted into broad-based human development.

Serenity and tranquillity therefore have relevance to African development at several levels.

At the individual level, citizens need resilience and responsible behaviour.

At the community level, traditional institutions, civil society organisations, religious bodies and local leaders can contribute to peaceful coexistence.

At the national level, governments must promote justice, accountability and inclusive development.

At the regional level, African states need cooperation in addressing insecurity, migration, climate change, trade and development.

1.7 Serenity, Leadership and Governance

Leadership has a particularly important relationship with serenity and tranquillity.

Leaders influence the emotional and behavioural climate of organisations and societies. Leaders who communicate irresponsibly, encourage hostility or exploit social divisions may intensify tension. Leaders who demonstrate restraint, fairness and respect can help calm difficult situations.

Good governance therefore involves more than administrative efficiency.

It includes:

competence + integrity + accountability + justice + inclusion + responsibility.

Where citizens believe that institutions are fair and responsive, trust is more likely to develop. Where institutions are perceived as arbitrary or corrupt, distrust can increase.

Institutional trust is consequently a major foundation of national tranquillity.

This is why meritocracy, accountability and the rule of law are not merely administrative concepts. They are also instruments of peacebuilding.

1.8 Serenity and the Management of Conflict

Conflict is inevitable wherever people have different interests, values and expectations.

The objective cannot therefore be to eliminate every disagreement.

The more realistic objective is to ensure that disagreements do not unnecessarily become destructive conflicts.

This requires developing the capacity to:

1. listen; 2. understand opposing perspectives; 3. distinguish interests from personalities; 4. communicate respectfully; 5. negotiate; 6. compromise where appropriate; 7. seek mediation when necessary; and 8. pursue justice through legitimate institutions.

These principles apply equally to families, workplaces, communities and governments.

A society that learns how to manage disagreement constructively is likely to be more resilient than one that attempts to suppress every difference.

This continuum does not suggest that development follows a simple linear path. Rather, it illustrates the interconnectedness of the different levels.

National instability can undermine personal serenity. Environmental degradation can produce community conflict. International conflict can affect national economies. Poverty can generate social tensions. Therefore, the relationship operates in both directions.

The individual and the global system are connected.

1.9 Towards a Culture of Serenity and Tranquillity

Building a culture of serenity and tranquillity requires deliberate action.

First, individuals must develop self-awareness.

People need to understand their emotions, values, strengths and weaknesses.

Second, families must become schools of peaceful coexistence.

Children learn attitudes towards disagreement, respect and responsibility largely through their social environments.

Third, educational institutions must teach both knowledge and character.

Education should prepare people not merely to earn incomes but also to become responsible citizens.

Fourth, communities must develop mechanisms for dialogue.

Traditional leaders, religious organisations, civil society and community associations can contribute significantly.

Fifth, governments must strengthen justice and accountability.

Citizens are more likely to trust institutions when they believe that rules apply fairly.

Sixth, economic policies must promote opportunity and inclusion.

Persistent poverty and exclusion can undermine social stability.

Seventh, nations must cooperate internationally.

Contemporary challenges such as climate change, pandemics, migration, terrorism, technological disruption and economic instability frequently transcend national borders.

1.10 Conclusion

Serenity and tranquillity are more than desirable personal qualities. They are important foundations of human wellbeing, social cohesion, good governance and sustainable development.

Serenity begins with the individual's capacity to maintain inner balance and respond responsibly to life's challenges. Tranquillity extends this capacity into relationships, communities and institutions.

Neither requires the absence of disagreement. Rather, both require the ability to manage differences constructively.

The central lesson is therefore clear:

Peaceful societies are not created merely by preventing violence. They are created by cultivating people, relationships and institutions capable of managing differences with justice, dignity, responsibility and restraint.

For Nigeria, Africa and the wider international community, this has profound implications. Sustainable development cannot be reduced to economic growth, infrastructure or technological advancement. It must include the creation of societies in which people can live with dignity, security, justice, opportunity and hope.

The journey towards such a society begins with the individual but does not end there.

Serenity within the person must become tranquillity within the community; tranquillity within communities must contribute to national peace; and national peace must support international cooperation and global sustainability.

That is the foundational proposition upon which the remaining chapters of this book are built.
