# Chapter Fifteen

## Two Complete Mock Examinations and Marking Guide

### Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

- practise a balanced range of English Language skills under examination conditions;
- use feedback and an answer key to identify areas for further study; and
- review your writing with a clear, consistent practice rubric.

### 15.1 How to use these mock examinations

Both papers in this chapter are original practice materials written for this handbook. They are not WAEC or NECO past questions, and their sections, marks and suggested pacing do not reproduce an official paper. Before an examination, follow the current instructions, syllabus, timetable and paper format issued by the relevant examination body.

Attempt each paper without using notes. Your teacher may set a suitable time limit for the whole paper or for individual sections. After finishing, use the answer key and marking guide. Record not only your score but also the reason for each error: a rule you did not know, a word you misunderstood, a question you misread, or a careless choice.

Each paper has five parts: objective language questions (10 marks), Oral English (5 marks), comprehension (10 marks), summary (10 marks) and continuous writing (25 marks). The total is 60 practice marks. This scoring system is for self-assessment in this handbook only; it is not an official WAEC or NECO allocation.

### Part C: Comprehension — 10 marks

Read the passage and answer the questions that follow.

When the old footbridge near Kola's community school became unsafe, pupils had to take a longer road to reach school. During the rainy season, the alternative path was muddy, and some pupils arrived late. The community's first meeting produced many suggestions but no clear plan. The head teacher then invited pupils, parents, a local builder and the community development association to inspect the bridge together.

The builder explained which parts needed attention and which materials were suitable. The association agreed to provide materials, while parents volunteered to organize transport. The pupils made posters asking road users to keep the work area clear. They were not allowed to handle tools or enter the work site; adults supervised the project and completed the repairs.

The work took several weekends. When the bridge reopened, the head teacher asked the pupils what they had learned. Kola said that the most useful lesson was not simply that people could repair a bridge. It was that a community made better decisions when it listened to people with different knowledge and agreed on responsibilities. The pupils also learned that volunteering did not mean doing unsafe work. Their role had been to share information and help adults coordinate safely.

1. Why did pupils have to use a longer route to school? (2)
2. Name two groups or individuals, apart from the pupils, who contributed to the project. (2)
3. What did the builder explain to the community? (2)
4. In the passage, what does **coordinate** mean? (2)
5. Give one lesson Kola said the pupils learned. (2)

### Part D: Summary — 10 marks

Read the passage. In **four concise points**, state the ways a student can prepare effectively for an English Language examination. Do not copy whole sentences. Use your own words where possible.

Students preparing for an English examination should begin by checking the syllabus and listing the topics they need to study. They should divide their available time into regular sessions so that grammar, vocabulary, reading, writing and oral practice all receive attention. Reading a variety of suitable texts can build vocabulary and help students notice how ideas are organized. However, reading alone is not enough: students should answer questions, write essays and summaries, and practise speaking or listening tasks where these are part of the examination. After each exercise, they should compare their answers with a reliable guide or discuss them with a teacher. Keeping a record of recurring mistakes helps students decide what to revise next. In the weeks before the examination, students can attempt mixed practice papers and learn to follow instructions carefully. They should also ask for help when a rule or question remains unclear. Last-minute memorisation without understanding is less useful than steady practice followed by correction.

### Part C: Comprehension — 10 marks

Read the passage and answer the questions that follow.

For several years, the students of Unity Senior School used the library mainly as a place to borrow set texts. The shelves contained useful books, but many students did not know how to find material for a project or decide whether a source was reliable. When their teacher assigned a project on clean water, some students copied the first paragraphs they found online. Others wrote notes but forgot to record where the information came from.

The librarian organized a short workshop. She showed the class how to use the catalogue, compare information from more than one source and write down the title, author and date of a source while taking notes. She also explained that a web page could be inaccurate or out of date. Students should check who produced it, when it was updated and whether another dependable source supports its claims.

The class then divided the project into questions: how water becomes contaminated, how communities can protect local sources, and where people can seek reliable public-health advice. Each group shared its findings and explained how it had checked them. The finished project did not contain the greatest number of facts. It was valued because the students selected relevant information, credited their sources and explained their ideas in their own words.

1. What difficulty did some students have before the workshop? (2)
2. Name two details the librarian asked students to record about a source. (2)
3. Give two checks students should make before relying on a web page. (2)
4. What does **credited** mean as used in the final paragraph? (2)
5. Why was the finished project valued? (2)

### Part D: Summary — 10 marks

In **four concise points**, state the advice the passage gives for checking and using information responsibly. Do not copy whole sentences.

Information is easier to use responsibly when a student first turns a broad topic into clear questions. The student should search for information that answers those questions rather than collect facts without a purpose. When using a book or web page, the student should note its author or organization, title and date. A web source should be checked for who produced it, when it was updated and whether another dependable source supports it. Important claims should be compared across sources because one page may contain errors or outdated information. While taking notes, students should distinguish the source's exact wording from their own explanation. They should not copy paragraphs and present them as their own work. Instead, they should select relevant points, explain them clearly in their own words and acknowledge the sources used. Before submitting a project, they should review whether each paragraph answers the topic and whether borrowed ideas have been credited.

For comprehension, award marks for an accurate answer that addresses the question and is supported by the passage. Accept equivalent wording. Do not require a candidate to reproduce this key word for word.

### Summary points

Award up to 2.5 marks for each distinct relevant point, to a maximum of 10. Accept concise paraphrases; do not award twice for the same idea.

**Paper One:** check the syllabus and plan topics; study regularly across skill areas; read varied texts; practise each skill; compare work with a reliable guide or teacher; record and review mistakes; practise mixed questions and follow instructions; ask for help; prefer steady understanding to last-minute memorisation. Any four distinct points earn full content credit.

**Paper Two:** turn the topic into clear questions; search for relevant information; record source details; assess the producer, date and currency of a web page; compare important claims across dependable sources; distinguish quotations from notes; explain ideas in one's own words; acknowledge sources; check that the final work answers the topic. Any four distinct points earn full content credit.

### Continuous-writing practice rubric — 25 marks

| Criterion | Marks | What to consider |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Content and task fulfilment | 8 | Relevant ideas; sufficient development; response fits the topic and purpose. |
| Organization | 5 | Clear opening and ending; logical sequence; effective paragraphing and linking. |
| Expression | 7 | Appropriate vocabulary; clear sentences; suitable tone and audience awareness. |
| Mechanical accuracy | 5 | Grammar, spelling and punctuation that allow the reader to understand the response. |

This is a handbook practice rubric, not a reproduction of an official marking scheme. For a letter or speech, also consider whether the conventions and tone suit the task. Award credit for original, relevant ideas; do not require the model wording in this chapter.

### Interpreting your result

Add the five section scores for a practice total out of 60. Use the result to decide what to revise; it does not predict an official grade. For every missed item, write down the correct answer and the reason it is correct. Ask a teacher to review any answer where the explanation remains unclear.
