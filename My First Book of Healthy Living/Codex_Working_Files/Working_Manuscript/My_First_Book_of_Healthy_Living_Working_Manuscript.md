## Preface

Good health is more than the absence of illness. It includes the way we care for our bodies, learn and think, understand our feelings, build relationships and live safely with other people.

Children learn healthy habits in ordinary moments: when they drink water after play, wash their hands before a meal, talk to a trusted adult about a worry, or make time for sleep. These habits grow best when children are supported rather than frightened or blamed. Families, teachers, caregivers, health workers and communities all have a part to play.

This book introduces ten areas of healthy living through short lessons, familiar situations, questions and activities. Some activities need an adult's help. Children should never use this book to diagnose an illness or decide which medicine to take. If something feels wrong, they should tell a trusted adult. Adults should seek qualified health advice when a child is ill or needs support.

I hope this book helps children make thoughtful choices and gives the adults around them useful ways to encourage those choices.

## A Note to Parents, Teachers and Caregivers

This book is written mainly for children around ages 7–12. A younger child may enjoy it with an adult. Children develop at different rates, and some health needs require individual advice. Please adapt activities for a child's age, ability, disability, culture and circumstances. Never shame a child for food, illness, feelings, family circumstances or a habit they are still learning.

The book offers general education. It does not replace a health professional's advice, diagnosis or treatment. Follow current guidance from qualified health workers and relevant Nigerian authorities, especially for immunisation, medicines and illness.

## How to Use This Book

Each chapter gives you a short lesson, a situation to think about, questions and an activity. You do not have to get every answer right. The situations and child characters in this working draft are fictional examples created to teach the ideas. The aim is to understand one useful idea and practise it safely. If an activity asks you to use food, water, exercise equipment or a digital device, ask an adult to help make it safe.

# CHAPTER ONE

## UNDERSTANDING HEALTHY LIVING

### What does it mean to be healthy?

A child may say, “I am healthy because I am not sick today.” Not being sick is good, but health is bigger than that. Health includes caring for the body, learning and thinking, understanding feelings, and relating well with other people.

These parts of health connect. If you are hungry, tired or worried, it may be harder to concentrate in class. If you have a problem with a friend, you may feel sad even when your body is well. When you receive care, rest and encouragement, it can be easier to learn and play.

Healthy living is not about being perfect. It means practising helpful habits, making safe choices, and asking for support when you need it. Children do not have to solve every health problem by themselves. Adults have important responsibilities too: to provide care, listen, keep children safe and seek professional help when needed.

### A morning for Ada

On Monday, Ada rushed to school. She had slept late, could not find her clean uniform and left home without breakfast. During the first lesson, she felt tired and kept thinking about the things she had forgotten.

At break time, Ada told her teacher she was having a difficult morning. The teacher listened. Together they made a simple plan: Ada would prepare her uniform and books before bedtime, and she would ask her caregiver about a quick breakfast she could manage in the morning.

The next day was not perfect, but it was easier. Ada learned that small routines can help—and that asking for help is a healthy choice.

### Four parts of well-being

- **Physical well-being** is caring for the body through food, movement, cleanliness, rest and health care.
- **Mental well-being** includes learning, remembering, asking questions and solving problems.
- **Emotional well-being** means noticing feelings and finding safe ways to express them.
- **Social well-being** grows through respectful relationships, cooperation and belonging.

No child feels cheerful every moment. Everyone has difficult days. The goal is not to hide feelings or pretend that problems do not exist. The goal is to notice what is happening and find support.

### Try this: My well-being wheel

Draw a circle and divide it into four parts: physical, mental, emotional and social well-being. In each part, draw or write one thing that helps you feel cared for. Then draw yourself practising three healthy habits. You may ask a trusted adult to help. You do not need to show your drawings to anyone unless you want to.

### Think about it

1. Why is health more than not being sick?
2. Which part of well-being would you like to care for this week?
3. Who is one safe adult you can speak with when you need help?

**Remember:** Healthy living is a practice, not a competition. Children deserve support while they learn.

**My promise:** I will care for myself, respect others and ask a trusted adult for help when I need it.

**New words:** well-being, physical, mental, emotional, social.

# CHAPTER TWO

## EATING WELL: FOOD THAT HELPS US GROW

### Food gives the body what it needs

Food gives the body energy and nutrients for growing, learning, moving and repairing itself. No single food contains everything the body needs. Eating different kinds of foods, when they are available, helps the body receive a wider range of nutrients. Many healthy diets are possible, and families choose food within their circumstances. [6]

Families choose foods for many reasons: what is available, what is affordable, culture, faith, allergies and health needs. A healthy meal does not have to look exactly like someone else's meal. Children should not tease or shame one another about food.

### A meal can be built in many ways

A meal might include a staple food such as rice, yam, maize, cassava or potatoes; a source of protein such as beans, eggs, fish, groundnuts or meat; and vegetables or fruit when available. Water is a good everyday drink. These are examples, not a rule that every plate must contain every item.

Breakfast is one possible way to begin the school day with food. Families may choose pap with milk and an egg, oatmeal with banana, boiled yam with vegetable sauce, or bread with egg and fruit. These are ideas, not requirements. If you often feel hungry at school, tell a trusted adult; children should not be blamed for food they cannot choose or afford.

Different nutrients have different jobs. Foods such as rice, maize, yam, cassava and potatoes provide carbohydrates, which are an important source of energy. Beans, eggs, fish, groundnuts and meat provide protein, which helps the body grow and repair itself. Fruits and vegetables provide vitamins, minerals and fibre. Oils, avocado and nuts contain fats that the body also uses; a varied diet matters more than naming one food as “good” or “bad.” Water helps the body function and is a sensible everyday drink.

Some children have allergies or health conditions that affect what they can eat. They should follow guidance from their caregivers and health professionals. No child should take supplements or follow a restrictive diet because of a suggestion in a book or online.

### Tobi helps prepare lunch

Tobi's class was planning a picnic. Each pupil was asked to suggest a food that could be shared safely. Tobi suggested boiled yam and beans. Amina suggested oranges. Their teacher reminded the class to wash their hands, keep food covered and check with adults about allergies.

When the picnic began, there were different foods on the mat. The pupils learned that variety can include many familiar dishes. They also learned that food safety and kindness matter: nobody should be pressured to eat a food that makes them unwell or uncomfortable.

### Everyday food-safety habits

- Wash your hands with soap and safe water before eating or helping prepare food.
- Wash fruit and vegetables as an adult has shown you.
- Keep food covered and follow an adult's advice about storing it.
- Do not eat food that smells or looks spoiled; tell an adult instead.
- Drink water from a safe source. If you are unsure, ask an adult.
- Never taste an unknown liquid, plant, powder or medicine.

Breakfast can help some children feel ready for the morning, but families have different routines and resources. If you are often hungry at school, tell a trusted adult. Food support is a community responsibility, not something for a child to feel ashamed about.

### Try this: A food-colour map

With an adult, draw a simple map of foods your family already eats. Add foods from different colours or groups—perhaps beans, greens, maize, eggs, pawpaw, fish or oranges. Circle one food you would like to learn more about. Do not buy anything or change a special diet without an adult's guidance.

### Think about it

1. Why does the body need food and water?
2. Name two different foods that can help provide protein.
3. What should you do if food looks spoiled or you are unsure whether it is safe?
4. How can you show respect when another child eats a different food?

**Remember:** Variety is helpful, and safe food matters. Families make choices within their circumstances.

**My promise:** I will try to learn about nutritious foods, drink safe water and ask an adult about food safety.

**New words:** nutrition, nutrients, variety, allergy, food safety.

# CHAPTER THREE

## EXERCISE, PLAY AND PHYSICAL FITNESS

### Movement belongs in every day

Running, dancing, walking, skipping, cycling, swimming, football and helping with active games are all ways to move. Exercise is one kind of physical activity; play can be active too. Children do not need a gym or expensive equipment to enjoy movement.

Moving can help build strong muscles and bones, support the heart and lungs, develop balance and coordination, support emotional well-being and make it easier to sleep. Team games also give children a chance to practise taking turns, encouraging one another and following shared rules.

For children and teenagers aged 5–17, the World Health Organization recommends an average of at least 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity across the week. This is a public-health guide, not a reason to punish or shame a child. Children with disabilities, health conditions or different abilities can be active in ways that suit them, with support from caregivers or health professionals. [1]

### The game changes

During break, Emeka wanted to play football. His friend Sade had an injured ankle and could not run. The group decided to change the game: some pupils passed the ball while seated, and others took turns keeping score and calling out team names. Everyone could join in safely.

The group learned that play does not have to look the same for every child. If an activity causes pain, dizziness or unusual difficulty breathing, stop and tell an adult. Do not pressure a friend to continue.

### Move safely

- Choose a safe place away from traffic, open drains, sharp objects and unsafe water. If an activity is vigorous, start gently and warm up in a way that feels comfortable.
- Wear suitable footwear when you have it, and use protective equipment for activities that need it.
- Drink safe water, especially in hot weather or after active play.
- Warm up gently before a vigorous game and take breaks when you need them.
- Follow a trusted adult's instructions around roads, swimming and sports equipment.

Screens can help us learn and communicate, but long periods sitting still can crowd out movement, sleep and face-to-face play. Take breaks and make room for activities that get the body moving. Ask a caregiver about household screen rules.

### Try this: Make a movement menu

Write or draw five ways to move that are safe where you live. Include one quiet or low-impact choice, such as stretching or a movement game. Ask a friend or adult which activity they would enjoy. Choose one and plan when you can try it. With a caregiver or teacher, make a simple weekly movement timetable that includes breaks and rest.

### Think about it

1. What kind of movement do you enjoy?
2. How can a game be changed so more children can join?
3. What should you do if you feel pain or unwell during activity?

**Remember:** Movement should be safe, enjoyable and suited to the child.

**My promise:** I will make time for movement and include others when we play.

**New words:** activity, exercise, coordination, fitness, ability.

# CHAPTER FOUR

## PERSONAL HYGIENE AND CLEANLINESS

### Clean habits protect health and dignity

Personal hygiene means caring for the body and keeping clean. Cleanliness can reduce the spread of some germs, but nobody should be mocked for being dirty, sick or unable to access water or supplies. Adults and communities have a responsibility to make clean water, soap and toilets available.

### When to wash your hands

Wash your hands with soap and water before preparing or eating food; after using the toilet; after coughing, sneezing or blowing your nose; after handling rubbish; and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Rub soap over both hands, including between the fingers and around the nails, then rinse with clean water. If water or soap is not available, tell an adult and follow the safe alternative they provide. [2]

### Caring for teeth, skin, hair and nails

Brush your teeth twice a day with fluoride toothpaste. A parent, caregiver or dental professional can help younger children use the right amount and brush thoroughly. Tell an adult if your teeth or gums hurt. A dentist can give advice suited to your age and needs. [3]

Bathe regularly with clean water and soap that is suitable for your skin. Dry yourself with a clean towel and wear clean clothes when available. Keep nails short and clean where possible. Do not share toothbrushes, towels or other personal-care items.

Hair and skin are different for different people. Styles, textures and care routines can vary by family and culture. Hygiene is about health and comfort, not about having one approved appearance.

### Keep shared places clean

At home and school, put rubbish in the right place, keep food covered and use toilets safely. Do not touch chemicals, broken glass, electrical wires or sharp objects. Ask an adult for help with cleaning tasks that may be dangerous.

### Try this: Handwashing teacher

With an adult, practise washing your hands slowly enough to cover both hands with soap and rub all surfaces. Then draw six steps that show how to wash hands properly, or teach the steps to a younger child using a drawing or a song. Do not waste water; follow the way your household or school safely collects and disposes of it.

### Think about it

1. Name three times when washing hands with soap is important.
2. Why should toothbrushes and towels not be shared?
3. What can pupils do to keep a classroom clean without handling dangerous rubbish?

**Remember:** Clean habits help, and access to water and supplies is a shared responsibility.

**My promise:** I will practise the hygiene habits I can and ask an adult when I need supplies or help.

**New words:** hygiene, germs, sanitation, fluoride, environment.

# CHAPTER FIVE

## PREVENTIVE HEALTHCARE: STAYING HEALTHY AND SAFE

### Asking for care is a strong choice

Preventive healthcare includes steps that help protect health and notice problems early. Children should attend health checks arranged by caregivers and tell an adult when they feel unwell, hurt or worried. A qualified health worker can assess symptoms and explain what care is needed.

Vaccines help protect people from certain serious diseases. Caregivers should ask a health worker or check the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA) for the current Nigerian immunisation schedule. [8] A child should never be blamed or frightened about a vaccine. It is an adult's job to arrange care and answer questions honestly.

### Medicines are not sweets

Only take medicine when a responsible adult and qualified health professional say it is right for you. Never take another person's medicine, share your medicine, or taste an unknown tablet, syrup, herb, supplement or powder. Use traditional preparations, herbs and supplements only when a responsible caregiver has checked with an appropriate health professional. If you find medicine, do not touch or swallow it; tell an adult. Keep medicines away from small children.

### Protect against mosquitoes

Mosquitoes can spread malaria. Families can reduce bites by using an insecticide-treated net correctly, following local health advice about mosquito prevention, and keeping the area around the home free from standing water where mosquitoes can breed. If a child develops a fever or feels very unwell, tell an adult promptly. A health worker—not a child or this book—should decide whether testing or treatment is needed. [4]

### Food, water, roads and play

- Drink water from a safe source. Ask an adult how water is made safe in your home or school.
- Do not play near fast-moving traffic, open drains, deep water, fire or building sites.
- Never touch electrical wires or unknown chemicals.
- Cross roads with a responsible adult where needed and use a safe crossing place.
- Ask an adult before using tools, sports equipment or household appliances.

### Kemi asks before acting

Kemi found a bottle in a cupboard. It had a bright label and smelled sweet. She remembered that medicine and cleaning liquids are not food. She left it where it was and called her aunt. Her aunt thanked her and moved it to a locked storage place.

Kemi did not need to identify the liquid herself. The safe choice was to avoid touching or tasting it and ask an adult.

### Try this: A safer-home walk

With a caregiver, walk through one room and name possible hazards—such as medicines within reach, a wet floor or an exposed wire. Let the adult decide how to fix each hazard. Children should not move dangerous items themselves. With the adult, make a family safety checklist of hazards to fix or avoid.

### Think about it

1. Who should decide which medicine a child takes?
2. What should you do if you find a tablet or unknown liquid?
3. Who can you tell if you have a fever, injury or feel unsafe?

**Remember:** Prevention matters, and children should get help from trusted adults and qualified health workers.

**My promise:** I will not take unknown medicines or substances, and I will tell an adult when I need health or safety help.

**New words:** prevention, immunisation, vaccine, mosquito net, healthcare.

# CHAPTER SIX

## MANAGING STRESS, FEELINGS AND EMOTIONS

### Feelings give us information

Everyone has feelings. You may feel happy when you play with a friend, nervous before a test, angry when something seems unfair, or sad when you miss someone. Feelings are not good or bad children. They are signals that something is happening inside us.

You can name a feeling without acting on it. “I feel angry” is different from hitting someone. “I feel worried” is different from having to face every worry alone. A child can speak respectfully and still say, “I did not like that.”

### When a problem feels heavy

Schoolwork, examinations, friendship trouble, family changes, bullying and too many activities can make a child feel under pressure. Feelings such as happiness, sadness, anger, fear, excitement and disappointment are part of life. If worry or sadness continues for a long time or makes daily activities difficult, tell a trusted adult and ask for help. [7] Sometimes a problem can be improved with a plan. Sometimes a child needs help from an adult or a qualified professional. There is no shame in either.

When you are upset, you could pause, breathe slowly if that feels comfortable, get a drink of water, draw, move your body, or talk to someone safe. These small actions may help with an ordinary difficult moment; they do not replace help from a caring adult or a health professional when distress continues.

### Bisi chooses to tell

Bisi had stopped enjoying break time. Two pupils were making jokes about her and asking her to keep quiet. At first, she thought she should handle it alone. Then she spoke to her older cousin, who listened without blaming her. Together they told a teacher Bisi trusted.

The teacher helped the pupils make a plan to keep Bisi safe and check in with her. Bisi did not have to solve the problem by fighting or keeping it secret. She learned that telling a safe adult can be a brave first step.

If the first adult does not listen or help, keep telling another trusted adult. If you feel unsafe, move to a safer place and seek help as soon as you can. No child deserves to be bullied or harmed.

### Friendship and self-control

A good friend respects your boundaries, does not pressure you to do unsafe things and can apologise after a mistake. Friends may disagree. You can take a break, explain what happened and ask a trusted adult to help settle a problem. You do not have to remain close to someone who repeatedly frightens or hurts you.

Self-control is not pretending you never feel angry. It is practising what to do with anger so nobody gets hurt. If you are afraid you might hurt yourself or another person, tell an adult right away.

### Try this: My support map

Draw four faces showing feelings such as happiness, sadness, anger and worry. Beside each, draw one safe way to respond. Then draw a small house with three windows. In each window, write or draw a safe adult you could speak to: perhaps a caregiver, teacher, relative, coach, community or faith leader, or health worker. Choose adults who listen and help keep children safe. If one person cannot help, think of another.

### Think about it

1. Name a feeling you have had this week. What helped you understand it?
2. What could you do if a friend is being bullied?
3. If the first adult you tell does not help, what can you do next?

**Remember:** Feelings can be shared safely. Asking for help is a strength.

**My promise:** I will try to express my feelings respectfully and seek a safe adult's help when a problem is too big to handle alone.

**New words:** feeling, stress, boundary, support, self-control.

# CHAPTER SEVEN

## SLEEP, REST AND RELAXATION

### Sleep helps the body and mind

Sleep gives children time to rest and supports healthy growth, learning, memory, attention and emotional balance. Children also need breaks during busy days. Rest can mean sleeping, but it can also mean quietly reading, sitting outside, drawing or pausing after an active game. Too little sleep may leave a child tired, irritable or less able to concentrate. If this happens often, tell a caregiver rather than trying to solve a sleep problem alone.

Children need different amounts of sleep at different ages. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 10–13 hours for children aged 3–5, including naps; 9–12 hours for ages 6–12; and 8–10 hours for ages 13–18. These are regular sleep durations over 24 hours, and individual health needs can vary. Families can ask a health professional for advice if a child has ongoing sleep problems. [5]

### A bedtime routine that works

A calm routine can help the body prepare for sleep. A routine might include packing a school bag, washing, brushing teeth, putting away a device, reading quietly, saying a prayer if that is part of the family's beliefs, and going to bed at a regular time. Homes differ, so a routine should fit the child's circumstances and the household's schedule. Avoid exciting screen activities just before bedtime where possible, and avoid caffeinated drinks such as cola or energy drinks. Children should not be blamed when noise, crowded rooms, illness or other circumstances make sleep difficult.

Exciting screens, noise, worries or uncomfortable sleeping conditions can make it harder to settle. If something at home is stopping you from sleeping safely or comfortably, tell a trusted adult. Do not blame yourself for a problem you cannot control.

### Kunle makes a small change

Kunle sometimes played games on a tablet until he was too tired to finish getting ready for bed. His aunt helped him choose a stopping time. They set an alarm together. Kunle still enjoyed his game, but he had time to wash, prepare his books and read a short story before bed.

The change did not make every night perfect. It gave Kunle a routine he could return to when evenings became busy.

### Try this: A bedtime path

Draw a bedtime timetable with steps that could help you get ready for sleep. Choose steps that are safe and realistic in your home. Show your plan to a caregiver and adjust it together. Do not write down private family details you do not want to share.

### Think about it

1. What can sleep help the body and brain do?
2. Which quiet activity helps you relax?
3. If you are often tired or cannot sleep, which adult could you tell?

**Remember:** Sleep is part of health. Children need adults to help make safe, restful routines possible.

**My promise:** I will give myself time to rest and tell an adult if sleep is difficult for me.

**New words:** sleep, rest, routine, relaxation, concentration.

# CHAPTER EIGHT

## BALANCING SCHOOL, CHORES, PLAY AND FAMILY LIFE

### A child needs more than one kind of time

Children need time to learn, play, rest and be with people who care for them. They may also help with small, safe household tasks. A balanced day leaves room for these different needs. It does not mean that every day follows a perfect timetable.

In this chapter, “chores” means small, age-appropriate tasks such as putting away one's school things or helping set the table with an adult nearby. It does not mean that children should do dangerous work, miss school, or take on adult duties. Adults are responsible for children's safety and care.

### Plan, then be flexible

A timetable can help when a child has several things to remember. It might show school, meals, homework, play, family time and sleep. Leave some open time too. A family may need to change the plan when someone is ill, a chore takes longer, or an unexpected event happens. Changing a plan is not failure.

Play is important. Children learn cooperation, imagination, movement and problem-solving through play. Reading, music, drawing, sports and quiet time can all be part of recreation. Family time may include talking, cooking, worship, a walk or simply sharing a meal.

### A timetable that was too full

Mariam had lessons after school on most days. She wanted to visit her friend, finish homework and help at home, but she often felt exhausted. She spoke with her caregiver. Together they looked at the week and chose one activity to pause. They also made a short homework period followed by a break.

Mariam's family learned that a plan should support a child, not fill every minute. Adults should notice when a child is overloaded and help make a healthier schedule.

### Digital balance

Phones, computers and television can help children learn and connect with others. They can also take time away from sleep, movement and face-to-face activities. Ask a caregiver about family rules for screen use. Do not share your address, passwords, private photographs or personal information with people online. If a message makes you uncomfortable, stop replying and tell a trusted adult.

### Try this: Build a flexible day

Draw a day with six spaces: school or learning, meals, homework, movement or play, family time, and sleep. Add one space marked “change or rest.” Ask an adult to help make the plan realistic. If you have too many tasks, the adult should help decide what can wait.

### Think about it

1. Why do children need time for play and rest as well as school?
2. What makes a household task safe and suitable for a child?
3. What can you do if your schedule feels too full?

**Remember:** A child's timetable should protect learning, safety, rest and relationships.

**My promise:** I will do the tasks that are right for my age and ask an adult for help if I feel overloaded or unsafe.

**New words:** responsibility, timetable, recreation, flexible, privacy.

# CHAPTER NINE

## DEVELOPING HEALTHY DAILY HABITS

### Small actions become routines

A habit is something we practise often. Healthy habits can include washing hands before eating, brushing teeth twice a day, drinking safe water, moving our bodies, sleeping regularly and asking for help when unwell. The earlier chapters explain why these habits matter. This chapter helps you choose a routine you can actually practise.

A simple morning routine might include washing, brushing teeth, eating what is available, getting dressed and checking school materials. Families may include prayer or another quiet moment according to their beliefs. No child can control every part of health. A child may not choose what food is available, whether there is water at school, or when the family can visit a clinic. Healthy living is not a test of whether a child is “good.” Families, schools, communities and public services all help make healthy choices possible.

### One habit at a time

Trying to change everything at once can feel difficult. Choose one small action. For example: “I will put my water bottle by my school bag tonight,” or “I will wash my hands before lunch.” If you forget, try again the next day. A missed day does not erase your effort.

### Zainab's seven-day practice

Zainab wanted to remember to brush her teeth before bed. Her caregiver helped her put her toothbrush beside the sink. They made a simple chart with seven spaces. Zainab marked a star each time she remembered. When she forgot, her caregiver did not scold her. They asked what might make it easier the next evening.

The chart was a reminder, not a score. Zainab gradually remembered more often, and her caregiver still helped her brush well.

### Try this: A kind habit tracker

Choose one habit you can practise with an adult's support. Make a seven-day chart. Use a check, a drawing or a blank space—do not use the chart to punish or compare children. At the end of the week, ask: What helped? What got in the way? What small change would make next week easier?

Possible habits include:

- Washing hands before eating.
- Brushing teeth in the morning and before bed.
- Taking a movement break after sitting for a long time.
- Preparing school materials before bedtime.
- Asking for help when a problem feels too big.

### Think about it

1. Which healthy habit would you like to remember more often?
2. What could help you practise it?
3. If you forget, how can you begin again without blaming yourself?

**Remember:** Habits grow with practice, support and patience—not shame.

**My promise:** I will choose one healthy habit to practise and ask for help when I need it.

**New words:** habit, routine, practice, patience, reminder.

# CHAPTER TEN

## PERSONAL WELL-BEING: BUILDING A HEALTHY FUTURE

### Caring for yourself and others

Personal well-being includes caring for your body, mind, feelings, relationships and safety. It also includes learning how your choices affect other people. A healthy community is built when children and adults show respect, solve problems peacefully, keep shared spaces clean and make sure everyone can take part.

You can help without being responsible for fixing every problem. Children may remind a friend to use a rubbish bin, invite someone into a game, or share a lesson about handwashing. Adults and community leaders must handle bigger safety, health and environmental problems.

### Respect, fairness and safety

Healthy relationships need kindness, honesty, listening, cooperation, understanding, forgiveness and respect for boundaries. People may have different languages, abilities, customs, faiths or ways of learning. Differences are not a reason to bully or exclude someone.

If anyone touches you, speaks to you or asks you to do something that makes you feel unsafe, tell a trusted adult. Never go with a stranger or accept an unsafe instruction just because someone asks. Tell an adult about bullying or harm. You can keep telling adults until someone helps. You are not to blame for another person's unsafe behaviour. Online, do not share personal information or meet someone you know only through the internet without a responsible adult's guidance.

### Our community garden

The pupils in a class noticed that a corner of their schoolyard collected litter. They did not pick up broken glass or unknown waste. They told their teacher. The teacher arranged for adults to clear the area safely. Afterward, pupils planted seedlings in containers with permission and took turns watering them.

The class learned that caring for the environment is teamwork. Children can help save water, avoid littering, care for plants or remind one another to use shared spaces respectfully. They should not handle dangerous waste or chemicals. Adults must provide permission, tools and protection.

### Be a health ambassador

A health ambassador encourages safe, helpful habits by example—washing hands, including others in play or sharing a lesson from this book. A child should not diagnose illness, tell friends to take medicine, or replace a health worker. Share health information with care and ask an adult when unsure.

### My healthy-future project

First draw your dream healthy home, school and community. Then choose one small project with an adult: make a safe handwashing reminder for the classroom, invite a classmate to join a game, create a family bedtime story night, or help keep a permitted garden tidy. Ask first, include people who want to join, and stop if something becomes unsafe.

### Think about it

1. What does well-being mean to you now?
2. How can you include someone who is left out?
3. What is one safe way you and an adult could help your school or community?

**Remember:** Children can contribute to healthier communities, and adults must keep children safe and supported.

**My promise:** I will care for my health, respect others and take part in safe actions that help my community.

**New words:** well-being, respect, boundary, community, contribute.

# CONCLUSION

## A HEALTHY LIFE GROWS ONE DAY AT A TIME

Healthy living is not one perfect meal, one long run or one day without a problem. It grows through many small choices and through the care people give one another.

You have explored food, movement, cleanliness, healthcare, feelings, rest, time, daily habits and community life. You have also learned that children do not have to manage health alone. Caregivers, teachers, health workers and community members have responsibilities too.

Some days will go according to plan. Other days will be difficult. When you forget a habit, you can practise again. When you feel unwell or unsafe, tell a trusted adult. When a friend is struggling, listen kindly and help them find an adult who can support them.

Every child deserves a safe place to learn, play, rest and grow. A healthier future is built when families, schools and communities work together.

## My Healthy Living Pledge

I will try to:

1. Eat a variety of nutritious foods when they are available to me.
2. Drink safe water and ask an adult when I am unsure.
3. Move and play in ways that are safe for me.
4. Practise personal hygiene and care for shared spaces.
5. Ask an adult or health worker for help when I need it.
6. Make time for sleep, rest and recreation.
7. Notice my feelings and express them safely.
8. Respect other people and their boundaries.
9. Tell a trusted adult if I feel unsafe or see someone being harmed.
10. Keep learning and begin again when a habit is difficult.

## Glossary

**Ability:** What a person can do, with or without support or adaptations.

**Boundary:** A limit that helps a person feel respected and safe.

**Community:** People who live, learn or work together in a place or group.

**Emotional well-being:** Understanding feelings and finding safe support when needed.

**Exercise:** Physical activity that is planned or repeated to support fitness.

**Fluoride:** A mineral used in many toothpastes to help protect teeth from decay.

**Germs:** Tiny organisms; some can cause illness and others do not.

**Habit:** An action a person practises regularly.

**Healthcare:** Support and services that protect and improve health.

**Hygiene:** Habits that help keep the body and surroundings clean.

**Immunisation:** Protection against certain diseases, often through vaccines.

**Nutrition:** How the body receives and uses nutrients from food.

**Physical activity:** Movement that uses energy, such as play, walking or sports.

**Preventive healthcare:** Steps that help prevent illness or notice health problems early.

**Rest:** Time to recover from activity; it may include sleep or quiet activities.

**Stress:** The body's and mind's response to a challenging situation.

**Well-being:** A person's physical, mental, emotional and social health.
